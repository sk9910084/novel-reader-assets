🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 1 · The Glass Observatory

Mara Ellison had watched the sky being sold in pieces, and she had said nothing, because saying something would have cost her the only job that let her look at it at all.

The sale had taken eleven years. First the eastern quadrant went to Meridian Cola, whose orbital billboards learned to bend sunlight into a bottle that poured itself across the dawn. Then the southern arc went to a logistics company, and the delivery drones wrote the company crest across the Milky Way every night at nine. By the time the Consortium auctioned the zenith itself — the last clean patch of dark directly overhead — Mara was twenty-nine and employed as a night-watch astronomer at the Glass Observatory, a job that consisted mostly of logging light pollution and pretending the telescopes were still for science.

They were not. The great refractor on the dome's crown had not been pointed at anything real in six years. Mara kept it polished anyway. On clear nights, when the advertising swarms throttled down for maintenance between three and four in the morning, she would swing the old instrument up through the gap in the billboards and look at actual stars, the way people used to. It was against the terms of her employment. It was the only reason she stayed.

On the night everything changed, the gap came at 3:12 a.m., and the sky through it was wrong.

Mara noticed it the way she noticed everything about the sky: without deciding to. The maintenance window had opened on schedule, the billboard lattice dimming quadrant by quadrant until the dome's slit framed a clean rectangle of dark. She had the refractor trained on the Cygnus field, running a routine calibration against a star catalog old enough to remember a pre-sale sky. The catalog said the star designated Cyg-4417 should be sitting at magnitude 4.1, steady as a metronome.

It was blinking.

Not twinkling — blinking. On, off. On, off. A rhythm with edges too sharp for atmosphere. Mara frowned and checked the tracking. The mount was true. She checked the photometer. The photometer agreed with her eyes: Cyg-4417 was occulting in a pattern, two seconds on, one second off, two on, three off. It looked, unmistakably, like a signal.

She laughed, once, because the alternative was worse. Then she swung the refractor to the next calibration star, Cyg-4421, four arcminutes east.

It was blinking too. Same rhythm. Two on, one off, two on, three off.

Mara's hands went cold on the brass fittings. She moved to a third star, then a fourth, then she abandoned the Cygnus field entirely and slewed the telescope to Lyra, to Draco, to a random patch of the northern sky she had never calibrated in her life. Every star she touched was blinking in the same pattern. Every star in the visible sky, as far as the old refractor could reach, was switching on and off like a row of harbor lights spelling something out.

She sat down on the observer's stool and did not get up for a long time.

There were explanations that were not insane. A Consortium test, some new advertising layer being trialed in the maintenance window, a prank by the orbital engineers. Mara told herself all of them, in order, and believed none of them, because she had worked the night watch for six years and she knew the texture of every kind of artificial light the sky could hold, and this was not artificial. This was starlight being interrupted — not replaced, not overlaid, but gated, the way a hand passes in front of a candle.

Something between her and the stars was switching them off and on. Something enormous.

At 3:47 the maintenance window began to close. The billboard lattice woke quadrant by quadrant, Meridian Cola's bottle pouring its endless dawn, the logistics crest stitching itself across the galaxy. The blinking stars drowned one by one in the returning glare. Mara stayed at the eyepiece until the last one went under, and then she sat in the humming dark of the dome and wrote down the pattern in her logbook, in pencil, because pencil could be erased and ink could not.

Two on. One off. Two on. Three off.

She stared at the numbers until they rearranged themselves in her head, the way they always did when she was tired. 2-1-2-3. Or, if you read the silences as well as the lights: long short long, short long short, the oldest rhythm in the world.

A heartbeat, she thought, and then immediately told herself not to be poetic. But the thought would not leave. It was a heartbeat drawn across the whole sky, in starlight, during the one hour of the night when no one was supposed to be watching.

Mara locked the dome at dawn and took the service elevator down through the Observatory's glass spine, past the gift shop that sold postcards of skies no one under forty had ever seen. In the lobby, the morning shift was arriving: interns with tablets, a Consortium liaison with a smile like a sealed envelope. Mara nodded at all of them and went home to her apartment on the forty-first floor, where the windows faced a billboard and she had long ago stopped opening the curtains.

She did not sleep. She sat at her kitchen table with the logbook open and a cheap star atlas she had bought as a student, and she worked the problem the way her professors had taught her: assume you are wrong, and try to prove it.

Hypothesis one: equipment malfunction. Disproven by the fact that she had seen the blinking with her naked eyes, standing on the dome's catwalk during the last ninety seconds of the window, watching a dozen stars wink out and back in perfect unison like a choir breathing.

Hypothesis two: a Consortium test. Plausible, except that the Consortium owned the sky and had no reason to hide its tests in a maintenance window, and every reason not to let a night-watch astronomer see them.

Hypothesis three: she was losing her mind. She considered this one seriously, because it was the only one that frightened her less than the alternative. She ran through the checklist — sleep deprivation, yes, but she had worked worse weeks; no new medication; no family history. She put a pin in it.

Hypothesis four: the stars were being occluded by something real, something between Earth and the stars, something large enough to gate starlight across the whole visible sky and precise enough to do it in a rhythm.

She wrote the words something large enough and then she stopped writing, because her hand was shaking.

At noon she called in sick, which she had never done in six years, and at two in the afternoon she did something she had not done since graduate school: she went to the public library on Harrow Street, the one with the paper books, and she asked the archivist for everything they had on the sky auctions. The archivist, a man old enough to remember constellations, looked at her for a long moment and then led her to a basement room full of boxes that smelled like dust and victory.

What Mara found in the third box made her sit down on the concrete floor.

It was a prospectus. Consortium Sky Auctions, Round One, eleven years ago. Lot descriptions, bidding increments, the legal definition of a quadrant. And on page forty, in a section titled Reserved Instruments, a single paragraph she read four times:

The Consortium reserves the right to maintain, in perpetuity and without public disclosure, such orbital instruments as are necessary for the administration of the sky. These instruments are not subject to auction and do not appear on any public registry.

Orbital instruments. Not subject to auction. Not on any public registry.

Mara closed the box. She walked back up through the library, past the paper books, out into the afternoon glare of a city whose sky belonged to eleven companies and one secret. In her pocket, her phone buzzed: a message from the Observatory. Night shift roster confirmed. Dome duty, 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. See you tonight, Mara.

She stood on the library steps and looked up. The daytime sky was the color of old television static, washed out by the billboard lattice even at noon. Somewhere above it, invisible in the glare, something was waiting for the maintenance window.

Two on. One off. Two on. Three off.

She had eleven hours until the gap opened again. Eleven hours to decide what she was going to do when the sky started talking, and she was the only one listening.

She went home and opened her curtains for the first time in four years, and she began to plan.
