The compass did not point north.

Mara discovered it at noon, in the thin autumn light of the workshop, when she finally made herself lift it from the bench where she had left it the night before. Her mother's compass, dented and dark with age, the one instrument in the shop she had always been forbidden to repair. For two years it had kept true time, its needle swinging faithfully to the earth's poles. Now the needle stood fixed and certain, aimed up and east, past the ceiling and the roof, toward the place where the moon had set that morning.

'It is not broken,' Orin said, though she had not asked. He sat by the fire with the blanket over his knees, watching her the way he watched a mechanism he had not yet opened. 'It has found something stronger than north.'

'It's pointing at the moon.'

'It is pointing at whatever is answering your grief.' He held out his hand, and she gave him the compass, and he turned it slowly in his trembling fingers. The needle did not waver by a hair. 'Think, Mara. For fifteen years the pull was your mother, your wanting of her. Two years ago the lullaby came home, and the pull settled, and the needle rested. Now the tooth has learned the song, and the pull has found a new answer. It answers from the river camp. It answers from the sky. And the needle, poor honest thing, believes it.'

Mara thought of the cradle humming in the dark beyond the walls, of her mother's lullaby rising out of a tooth of solid grief, and felt the workshop tilt very slightly beneath her feet. 'Then it isn't only listening to me. It's calling me.'

'It is calling everyone whose grief it has tasted,' Orin said. 'But it learned your song first, and yours is the loudest in the room. That is either a weapon, Mara, or a leash. We had better find out which, before the full moon.'

The Queen received them in the small council chamber, without ceremony, in the gray dress she had worn for two years. She listened to Mara's account of the camp without moving, her hands folded on the table, and when Mara laid the warm brass token on the polished wood between them, the Queen looked at the gear-tooth stamp for a long time.

'The melt fell short by one chest,' she said at last. 'I was told it was a counting error. I chose to believe it, because believing it was easier than hunting my own treasury.' Her mouth twisted. 'Debt upon debt. Master Pell, the mint-master, kept the die and the short chest both. He will be in the cells by nightfall.'

'Varric's people are not criminals, Your Majesty,' Cael said quietly. He stood straight in his laborer's coat, the bridge dust still on his boots. 'They are mourners. They queue at dusk with their worst days wrapped in handkerchiefs like market goods. If you take Varric in chains, you will make him a saint, and the grief will find another shepherd by week's end.'

The Queen studied him, and something in her face softened and hardened at once. 'Then what would you have me do, bridge-builder?'

'Give me three days,' Mara said. 'Until the full moon. If we cannot starve the tooth by then, you may send your guards, and I will not stand in their way.'

The Queen nodded slowly. 'Three days. But Mara —' She rose, and for a moment she was not a queen at all, only a woman who had once laid her own grief into a machine and watched it grow teeth. 'Do not let it learn your voice the way mine learned his name. Grief that knows your name can call you home.'

Petra's rooms above the wash-house smelled of lye and rain. The laundress opened the door with the bulb from the fair still clutched in one hand, and behind her on the table stood the row of bulbs, six of them, dimmer than before, guttering like candles in a draft.

'They queue at dusk, Keeper,' Petra whispered, when Mara asked. 'Half of Tanners' Row has sold something now. Widow Lark sold the morning her husband died — she said she was tired of waking to it. Old Fen sold the winter his shop burned. They say the borrowed joy gets them through the cold months, and the grief —' She looked down at her hands. 'The grief was only weighing them down anyway. That's what the woman at the stall says. Grief is the one coin that never spends itself, but what good is a coin you never spend?'

Mara sat with her for an hour and listened, and said nothing about the threads or the moon, because Petra's hollow places were not a lecture to be delivered. At the end Petra pressed one of the bulbs into Mara's hands — the largest, the wedding day.

'Take it,' she said. 'The joy in it is fading anyway. Pour the rest back into me, Keeper. Pour it back so Tomas has a face again when I reach for him.'

Mara carried the bulb home wrapped in her handkerchief, and did not sleep, and at midnight she and Cael walked past the last lamp of the outer walls to the river camp, the bulb warm in her pocket and the Lunar Key cold against her wrist.

The fire burned too steady in the windless dark. The cradle of brass and cable hummed its low note, and the black tooth inside it had grown since they had last seen it — no longer a knuckle but a fist, matte and lightless, pulsing like a second heart. Ranked on the wagon shelves, the harvested griefs glowed darkly, hundreds of them, and the air above the camp seemed to tremble, as if something enormous were breathing just out of sight.

Varric looked up from the cradle as they approached, unsurprised. 'You brought her grief back,' he said, nodding at Mara's pocket. 'Pour it out, then. Let me show you what happens.'

'Pour it back into her,' Mara said. 'Not out. Into her.'

'Gently, Keeper.' Varric held out his hand, and after a moment Mara gave him the bulb. He held it above the cradle, where the tooth pulsed and waited. 'Watch the sky.'

He cracked the bulb between his palms.

The dark mist that rose from the glass did not drift toward Tanners' Row. It rose straight up, drawn like smoke up a chimney, and high above the camp the silver threads — the threads that tied every heart in Lume to the moon — trembled and bent downward to meet it, drinking the grief in long slow pulls. The tooth in the cradle pulsed brighter, stronger, and its hum deepened, shaped itself, became almost a voice.

And then it sang.

One line of the lullaby, low and sweet, in her mother's voice. Then a second line. Then the last line — the last line, the one Mara had never been able to remember, the one that had been lost to her for fifteen years, coming home now out of a tooth of solid grief, perfect and unbearable.

'It remembers the whole song,' Mara whispered, and her knees went weak, and Cael caught her elbow. 'It remembers more of her than I do.'

Petra's wedding day was gone — drunk by the threads, fed to the tooth, never to find its way back to Tanners' Row. And somewhere above them, past the trembling threads, the real moon turned in its slow majesty, and at its rim the black tooth grew by a fraction, and Mara felt it through the soles of her feet, a lurch and a wrongness she knew too well.

'Three nights,' Cael said as they walked home under the paling sky. His voice was very quiet. 'The astronomer measured it. By the full moon it will be whole. And then, Mara —'

'Then it goes home,' Mara said.

Above them the moon turned, and at its rim the black spot pulsed in a rhythm she felt in her own wrist, keeping time with her heart. And in her pocket, her mother's compass pointed at the sky, unwavering, certain, the way a needle points at the only thing it has ever wanted.
