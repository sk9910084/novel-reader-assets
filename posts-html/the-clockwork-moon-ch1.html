🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 1 · The Clockwork Moon

Mara Vale had always loved the hour after midnight, when the workshop belonged to her alone. The clocks on the walls ticked in their patient chorus, the oil lamps burned low and amber, and the whole city of Lume seemed to hold its breath between one day and the next. She sat at the long oak bench with a sailor's compass open before her, its tiny brass moon no bigger than a fingernail, and coaxed its mechanism with tweezers while the river murmured beyond the shutters. On the shelf above her workbench sat her mother's compass, dented and dark with age, the one instrument in the shop she was forbidden to repair. "Some things," Master Orin always said, "are meant to keep their own time."

Orin had vanished that afternoon. He had not come down for supper, had not left word with the chandler or the lamp-lighter, and when Mara searched the workshop she found his coat still hanging by the door and a folded note beneath her workbench, weighted with a gear the size of a coin. If night becomes a cage, follow the smallest gear. She had read it a dozen times. Orin never wrote riddles without reason, and he never vanished without worse reason. She had spent the evening telling herself he was merely at the observatory, arguing with the astronomer as he loved to do, but her hands kept returning to the note, and the workshop felt too quiet, as if the clocks themselves were listening.

At midnight, every clock in Lume struck once and stopped.

Mara felt it before she understood it: a single deep note from the cathedral bell, answered by a hundred smaller voices across the rooftops, and then a silence so complete it seemed to have weight. The pendulums on the workshop walls halted mid-swing. The great regulator in the corner froze with its hands at twelve. Even the water clock in the courtyard ceased its dripping, the last drop hanging at the spout like a held breath. Outside, the moon hung above the eastern roofs, bright and perfectly motionless, and the river beneath it had gone still as a ribbon of poured silver, not a ripple breaking its face.

She ran to the window. Across the street, Chandler Bem was standing in his doorway in his nightshirt, staring upward. Doors were opening all along the lane. Someone shouted that the bell-ringers had fallen asleep at their ropes, but the bell-ringers were shouting too, from the cathedral steps, pointing at a sky that refused to change. Mara looked down at the sailor's compass in her hand and felt her blood go cold. The tiny brass moon inside the instrument, which should have mirrored the real moon's slow turning, was spinning backward, faster and faster, as if time itself were unwinding inside the glass.

Then the screaming started, and Mara understood that this was not a dream.

People poured into the streets in their nightclothes, carrying lanterns that burned too steady in the windless air. The bakers of Flour Row stood outside their shops, flour on their hands, waiting for a dawn that did not come. Old Hettie, who sold river eels, sat down in the middle of the lane and wept into her apron. Watchmen walked their rounds in confusion, staring at shadows that refused to move, their own shadows stretching behind them like spilled ink, frozen at the angle of midnight. A child asked his mother why the stars were not walking, and the mother had no answer, only her arms around him.

Mara was halfway down the stairs with her coat half-buttoned when she saw it: a thin golden ladder unfolding from the face of the real moon, segment by segment, impossibly delicate, descending through the still air until its lowest rung touched the dome of the observatory on Scholars' Hill. The crowd saw it too. A great gasp went up from the streets, and then a strange quiet, the quiet of a thousand people deciding at once not to believe their eyes. But the ladder was there, gleaming, swaying almost imperceptibly, and Mara thought of Orin's note with a lurch like a missed stair. If night becomes a cage, follow the smallest gear.

She ran. Through streets gone strange and silent, past the bakers and the watchmen and the weeping eel-seller, up Scholars' Hill where the observatory dome rose pale against the frozen sky. The ladder's lowest rung hovered just above the dome's copper ribs, close enough to reach. Mara seized it with both hands and found it warm, warm as bread, warm as sunlight, though the night around her was cold. She climbed.

The city fell away beneath her: the silver river, the dark roofs, the tiny lanterns like fallen stars. The rungs did not creak. The air grew thin and tasted of ozone and old paper, and twice she had to stop and press her forehead against a rung until the dizziness passed. Below, Lume looked like a clockmaker's model, perfect and stopped. Above, the moon loomed larger with every rung until it filled the sky, and she saw what the astronomers had never seen: it was not a world of dust and crater, but an enormous clock face, silver and engraved, turning with geological slowness around a dark central axle. And there, jammed between two great silver teeth at the rim, was a gear of pure black, matte and lightless, holding the whole mechanism still.

The observatory door stood open. Inside, the city astronomer lay unconscious beside the great telescope, his cheek against the cold floor, breathing shallowly. Mara righted the telescope with shaking hands and looked through it, and the clock-face moon leapt into terrible clarity: gears within gears, chains of stars, and the black gear wedged fast, vibrating faintly, as if something inside it were struggling.

A flutter of wings made her turn. Down the golden ladder came a mechanical bird, no bigger than a sparrow, its feathers hammered copper, its eyes two bright sapphires. In its beak it carried a key of dark iron, intricately toothed. It landed on the telescope's brass rail, regarded Mara with those jewel eyes, and dropped the key into her open palm. Then it spoke, and the voice that came from its tiny breast was Master Orin's voice, thin and urgent: "Do not let the Crown wind it."

"Orin?" Mara whispered. "Where are you? What is this?"

"The moon is a clock," said the bird, "and the Crown has stopped it. The key winds backward or sets free. Choose when you must. Do not let the Crown choose." Its sapphire eyes dimmed, and it folded its wings, suddenly only a beautiful machine again.

Boots rang on the observatory steps. Royal guards filled the doorway, lanterns blazing, and at their head a young captain, barely older than Mara herself, with soot on his cheek and fear undisguised in his eyes. "By order of the Queen," he began, and his voice cracked, "every clockmaker in Lume is summoned to the palace. At once." He looked at the golden ladder, at the unconscious astronomer, at Mara standing by the telescope with her fist closed, and Mara saw him decide, visibly, not to ask what he did not want to know. "I'm Captain Cael Arden," he said more quietly. "Please. Come quickly."

Mara slid the iron key into her sleeve, where it lay cold against her wrist, and followed him down the hill. She counted the unmoving minutes by habit, as Orin had taught her, one for every sixty heartbeats, though her heart was beating far too fast. In the palace courtyard stood a machine shaped like a tower-sized pocket watch, brass and iron, its face open to show wheels within wheels. Thick cables ran from its crown upward, vanishing into the frozen sky toward the moon. Engineers swarmed over it like ants, shouting, and from the palace balcony the Queen herself looked down, small and straight in her mourning black.

"Citizens of Lume," the Queen called, and the courtyard fell silent. "Our city has suffered enough. Accidents. Losses. Wrong choices that cannot be unmade." Her voice carried clearly in the windless air. "At dawn—if dawn can be restored—I will turn this city back by one full year, and we will have our dead again, and our mistakes undone." A murmur ran through the crowd, half hope, half horror. Mara's fingers closed around the key in her sleeve until its teeth bit her palm. She understood Orin's warning now, completely and terribly. The moon had not broken by itself. Someone had stopped it, deliberately, to make time itself obey. And the Crown meant to wind it.
