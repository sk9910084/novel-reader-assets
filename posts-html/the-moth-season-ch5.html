🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 5 · The Moth Season

Ruth stood in the middle of the empty marsh road and watched the last thin ribbon of the swarm pour itself through the dark of the farmhouse door, moth by moth, like water finding a drain. Then the road was empty, the sky was empty, and the only light in all of Harrow Farm was the lamp burning in the upstairs window.

She had expected the door to be locked. It was not. It swung inward at her touch without a sound, and the smell came out to meet her — damp plaster, old smoke, and something sweeter underneath, like honey gone dark in the comb. The hall was grey with moths. They covered the walls in a still, breathing wallpaper, every one of them facing her, and as she stepped inside they parted around her the way they had parted around her lamp on the cliff path — a crowd that knew her name and was deciding whether to say it.

The stairs were at the back. She climbed them with Eda's — no, with the stranger's — lamp-smell in her nose, her own father's lamp cold in her fist. She had not lit it. Something in her said that two lamps in that house would be one too many.

The upstairs room faced the marsh. The lamp stood on a table by the window, burning low and steady, and beside it in the narrow bed lay a woman so old and thin that Ruth thought for a moment she was looking at a bundle of clothes — until the bundle turned its head, and the eyes found her, and Ruth knew her.

Everyone in Dunmere knew Eda Harrow, the way you know a weather vane. She had not been seen in town in thirty years, and the town had long ago decided Harrow Farm was empty, the way towns decide things that frighten them. But Ruth had been twenty-two and home from college the winter Eda's husband died, and she remembered the funeral: one mourner, upright as a fence post, who had not cried.

"Mrs. Harrow."

"Ruth Adler." The voice was a dry leaf scraping stone. "The swarm spelled your name, so I knew you'd come. Shut the door, child. They don't like the draught."

Ruth shut the door. The moths on the walls settled deeper into their stillness.

"You called it," Ruth said. It was not a question.

"A lamp in the window," Eda said, "and a name called into the dark. My mother taught me. Her mother taught her. The women in my family have always known how to call things." She lifted one wasted hand and let it fall. "I'm dying, Ruth. The doctor in town doesn't know — I haven't let a doctor past that door in forty years — but I know. Weeks, now. Maybe days. And I called the swarm because there is something I have to see before I go."

"Your son."

Eda's eyes filled, sudden as rain. "Tommy. Sixty years I've waited. Sixty years since the swarm spelled his name on the schoolhouse wall and I — " Her voice broke. "I answered for him, Ruth. Do you understand? A mother can answer for her child. Three for yes. I knocked three times on my own bedroom wall, and by Christmas he was gone, and I have carried that knock inside my chest every day since."

Ruth sat down slowly on the room's one chair. The moths on the ceiling shifted, a grey tide turning in its sleep.

"It doesn't bring them back," Ruth said quietly. "The dead. It doesn't ferry anyone anywhere, does it?"

"No." Eda's smile was terrible. "It names a price. I called for my boy, and it came three years early, and it spelled your name on your window — not his, not mine. Yours." She looked at Ruth steadily. "I owe you an apology for that. And an explanation."

"I'm listening."

"When you call the swarm, it asks for a living name. That's the arithmetic. I called for the dead, so it demanded the living." Eda's fingers plucked at the blanket. "I gave it yours. In the dark, calling names, I found myself saying the name of the woman on the cliff — the beekeeper the town says fears nothing. I told myself it was because you were the strongest name I knew. Someone the swarm couldn't break. Someone who had already refused it once and lived."

Ruth went very still. "You knew I refused."

"The whole marsh knows, child. The night it knocked for you, three and three, and you sat in your dark room and said nothing — every moth from here to the headland felt it. Nothing has ever refused it before. Nothing." Eda leaned forward, and the moths on the wall leaned with her. "That's why it went to Nora. That's why it went to Ada. Don't you see? It collects substitutes. It wanted you, and you wouldn't come, so it took the names in order of your love — the people you'd answer for. It was always going to come back to you. It always comes back to the one who refuses. Refusal is only a delayed yes."

"Unless you mean it," Ruth said.

Eda's eyes sharpened. "Unless you mean it."

Silence. The lamp burned. Outside, the marsh lay grey and patient under the thinning mist.

"Tonight," Eda said, "it will knock for you at the marsh gate, where it nests. It will come to your window no more. It wants the answer out where it lives." She reached under her blanket and brought out a small brass lamp, old and dented, its handle worn smooth. "Take my lamp. The moths can't help but go to a light, and this one knows the way better than your father's does."

Ruth took the lamp. It was warm, as if it had been burning for years and had only just now been set down.

"What do I do?" Ruth asked.

"You answer it. Once." Eda raised one finger. "One knock. That's no, in the old code. Three for yes, one for no. And Ruth — " her hand closed around Ruth's wrist with surprising strength — "you must mean it the way you have never meant anything. Not with anger. Not with fear. It feeds on both; anger is only fear with its coat on. Answer with the emptiness of a woman who has nothing left it can take. Do you understand? It took your bees. It frightened your sister. It nearly took Ada. Let it have the anger for those things. Give it away. Walk in there with nothing in your hands."

"And the price?" Ruth whispered. "What does no cost?"

Eda lay back against her pillows, and all at once she looked not old but finished, the way a candle looks just before it gutters. "Me," she said simply. "That's the bargain I made when I called it. I go willingly tonight — to wherever the taken go, to my boy — and the swarm releases Dunmere. Sixty years I've owed it an answer, and tonight I'll give it. But a bargain needs a witness, Ruth. Someone living, who looks it in the face and refuses it on behalf of all of us. Say no for the town the way I should have said it for Tommy."

On the ceiling, the moths began to lift — not scattering, but unbinding, a slow grey spiral folding itself toward the window, toward the marsh. Eda watched them go with something like tenderness.

"He's calling me, Ruthie," she whispered, and smiled, gentle as dusk. "I have to answer."

Ruth stood. Her legs did not want to hold her. At the door she stopped and looked back, and Eda Harrow raised one wasted hand in farewell, and the lamp on the table burned on, and the last of the moths poured out through the window into the grey afternoon like breath leaving a body.

Ruth walked down the stairs and out into the road. The sun was lowering, and the mist was coming off the marsh in banks, and ahead of her, where the road met the reed beds at the marsh gate, the moths were gathering — hundreds of them, settling onto the wet stones, wing to wing, perfectly still.

She read the shape they made from thirty yards away, and her blood went cold and then, strangely, calm.

One word. Four letters, in letters a hand's width tall.

COME.
