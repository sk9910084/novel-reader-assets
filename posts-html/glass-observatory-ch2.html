🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 2 · The Glass Observatory

The curtains stayed open all afternoon, and the billboard across the street filled Mara's apartment with a slow, breathing light, like something enormous trying to learn how to sleep.

She worked the way she had been taught to work when the data refused to behave: she made it smaller. She took the pattern — two on, one off, two on, three off — and wrote it as a string of ones and zeroes on the back of a delivery receipt. 1101110. She stared at it. Seven bits. Too short for anything, too regular for nothing.

She tried Morse. She tried primes. She tried reading the gaps instead of the lights, and at half past six she put the pencil down and admitted the truth she had been circling all afternoon: seven bits was not the message. Seven bits was a metronome. The message was whatever the metronome was keeping time for.

Whatever the sky was saying, it was saying it longer than ninety seconds. She had only ever caught the first bars through the maintenance window, before the billboards drowned it out. To hear the rest, she needed the sky for longer than the Consortium allowed anyone to have it.

That was the problem she spent the evening solving, and the solution, when it came, was so obvious she laughed out loud in her empty kitchen.

The Observatory had a second telescope.

Everyone forgot the second telescope, because the Glass Observatory had been designed to be looked at, not to look through. The great refractor on the dome's crown was the showpiece, the one on the postcards. But down in the service basement, behind the water heaters, there was a decommissioned survey instrument from the pre-sale era — a clunky equatorial with a cracked corrector plate, donated thirty years ago and never officially removed from the inventory. Mara had found it in her first month, cleaned its lenses out of boredom on a slow night, and never told anyone.

It had no connection to the Consortium network. No photometer. No logging software. Nothing that could phone home.

But it had an eyepiece, and a clock drive she could crank by hand, and most importantly, it had a view. The basement had a light well — a narrow shaft of glass running the height of the Observatory's spine, installed by an architect who had wanted daylight in the service corridors. It faced straight up. At night it framed a handkerchief of sky, small and useless for science, invisible to anyone who didn't know it was there.

A handkerchief was enough. The blinking had covered the whole visible sky. Any patch would do.

Mara packed her bag at nine: the star atlas, the logbook, a thermos, a stopwatch she had owned since university, and the cracked corrector plate wrapped in a towel. She took the service elevator to the basement and let herself in behind the water heaters with the key she had cut in her first year, telling the locksmith it was for a storage locker.

The telescope was where she had left it, under a dust sheet, patient as a held breath. She uncovered it, reassembled the finder, and cranked the clock drive until it ticked. The light well above was a gray rectangle of billboard wash — the lattice at full brightness, the night's advertisements already running. She would have to wait for the maintenance window like everyone else. The difference was that nobody knew she would be watching.

She waited by counting. The water heaters ticked. Somewhere above, the night shift began its rounds. At eleven, the liaison's voice drifted down the service stairwell — one of his unannounced inspections, the kind that were really reminders of who owned the building. Mara sat very still in the dark behind the heaters until his footsteps went back up.

At 3:12 a.m., the billboard lattice dimmed.

Mara had the survey scope trained on the light well and her stopwatch in her fist before the last quadrant went dark. She found a bright star — she didn't know which one, the well was too narrow for the atlas, and it didn't matter — and she started counting. She had decided, in the afternoon, that she would not try to decode the message in her head. She would do what astronomers had done for centuries before computers: she would record. One for light, zero for dark, in pencil, one column, for as long as the window held.

The star blinked. She wrote.

1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0. The metronome, exactly as before — the heartbeat that opened every cycle. Then the pattern changed. The blinks came faster, uneven, grouped: three quick, a pause, two slow, a pause, a long run of rapid flickers like a held breath let go all at once. Mara's pencil flew. Her hand cramped. She switched hands and kept writing, and the star kept talking, and nobody in the whole glass spine of the Observatory knew that thirty meters above them, the night-watch astronomer was taking dictation from the sky.

At 3:47 the lattice woke. The star drowned in returning glare. Mara sat back, her palm black with graphite, and looked at what she had.

Four pages of ones and zeroes. She counted them twice: 1,847 bits.

She did the arithmetic on the back of the fourth page. 1,847 bits, at the rate she had recorded them, meant the message was cycling on a loop roughly eleven minutes long. She had caught almost the whole of one loop. The seven-bit heartbeat wasn't the message. It was the loop marker. The sky was saying: start here.

She folded the pages into her jacket and went back up to the dome for the rest of her shift, logged light pollution with a steady hand, nodded at the liaison's sealed-envelope smile at dawn, and went home and slept three hours with the pages under her pillow.

Decoding took the rest of the day and most of her sanity.

She tried everything she had tried the day before, on a thousand times more data, and everything failed the way small things fail when the real thing is bigger. Then, at dusk, sitting on her kitchen floor with the pages spread around her like a paper constellation, she remembered something her signals professor had said twenty years ago: "If you ever find a signal you can't decode, stop asking what it says. Ask who it's for."

Who was it for.

Mara looked at the pages. 1,847 bits. She did the division she should have done hours ago: 1,847 was prime. You couldn't arrange a prime number of bits into a rectangle. A rectangle was how you sent a picture. A prime was how you sent a —

She went very still.

A prime was how you sent a location. Not a picture. A coordinate. A string of bits with no factors was a line, not an image — a one-dimensional thing. And the only one-dimensional message worth broadcasting from orbit, on a loop, during the one hour of the night when the billboard lattice went dark, was a set of numbers. A position. A where.

She was on her feet before she knew she was moving. She knew the conversion the way she knew her own name — binary to decimal, decimal to degrees, the old pre-sale notation every astronomer learned and no one used anymore, because the sky had been sold and coordinates had become property.

Her hands shook so badly she had to write the numbers three times. When she finished, she stared at the result, walked to the window, looked out at the billboard breathing its slow light across the street, and said the coordinates out loud to her empty apartment, because saying them made them real and she needed them to be real.

They were ground coordinates. Forty kilometers north of the city. An empty quadrant of scrubland never developed, because the Consortium had bought the mineral rights decades ago and never drilled.

The sky wasn't broadcasting to astronomers. It was broadcasting to someone standing in an empty field, forty kilometers north, waiting for instructions.

Mara folded the pages back into her jacket. She looked at the clock. Nine hours until the maintenance window. Nine hours to decide what you did when you found out the secret instruments the Consortium had reserved in perpetuity weren't watching the sky at all.

They were talking to the ground.

And whatever was listening out there in the scrubland had been listening for eleven years, in the dark, while the rest of the world watched advertisements.

She opened her curtains wider, and she began to plan the drive north.
