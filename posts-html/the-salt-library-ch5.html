The pen touched the paper, and the ink drank it.

Maya had expected to feel her mother's hand moving through her again, the way the chalk word LIGHT had appeared on the lamp housing without her choosing it. But the name that formed in the third column was in her own handwriting — the neat, mainland hand of the diary she had kept since she was nine, the hand that believed plans mattered.

MAYA RANE.

She stared at it. The ink was still wet. And then, beneath it, the ledger wrote for itself in the sea's wet-looking hand, the way it had written her name the night before: KEEPER. WILLING. And then the price, in full, exactly as her mother had spoken it across the table — the light, every night, for life; no leaving Gullpoint, not for a day, not for an hour; and no next name, the line ending here.

Above her the crank faltered — caught — and held its breath one last time.

Then it stopped.

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on the cottage walls and on Maya's ears, and when she looked up, the chair across the table was empty. The ledger lay closed. The lamp on the table was cold, its glass fogged with the memory of a flame. The sitting room smelled of damp paper and kerosene again, the way it had when she first walked in.

Her mother was gone.

Maya sat a long time listening to the new silence. She had done it. She had written her name. And yet nothing in the world had changed — the brass clock still said 4:17, the sea still breathed against the rocks, and the cottage was still dark. The price was named, but it was not yet paid. That part would come tomorrow night, when the Meridian Star rounded the point and Maya would have to climb the tower and turn the great lens by her own hand, and hold it turning, while the Board's order said DARK.

She slept on the sitting-room floor with the ledger under her cheek, the way a child sleeps with a book, and woke at dawn to the sound of someone knocking on the cottage door. Three times. Slow. Deliberate.

Her blood went cold. But when she opened the door, it was only Tomas, grey-faced, his oilskin buttoned wrong.

"The ferry came early," he said. "There's a man from the Board on it. Harrow."

The name hit her like a wave. Harrow — the signature on the 1996 letter, the usual rate. "What does he want?"

"He wants the lamp room sealed. The Board got wind that someone's been turning the light — fishermen saw the beam two nights running, and the cable was cut, so it can't be the Board's power." Tomas's eyes went to the open hatch in the sitting-room floor, to the ladder going down. "He'll want the crank gone too. He's brought two men and a torch for the housing bolts."

Maya's hand tightened on the doorframe. Without the crank, there was no lamp. Without the lamp, the Meridian Star died on the reef with three hundred souls, and every word she had written in the third column was nothing but ink on paper — and the sea, being exact, would collect anyway.

"Where is he now?"

"Harbour office. He'll be here by noon." Tomas hesitated. "Girl — there's something I never told you. Something about the third column."

"What?"

He looked at the grey morning sea. "The winter of '74. Your grandmother kept the dark for a freighter the Board had sold to the reef, and your grandfather — her husband, the keeper — couldn't bear it. He took the harbor skiff out into the fog and tried to warn the ship himself." Tomas's voice roughened. "The sea took him. And the ledger says SPARED — THE HARBOURMASTER'S BOY. The boy the sea spared that night, in exchange for the keeper who spent himself — was mine."

Maya stared. "Your son."

"Daniel." Tomas's jaw worked. "Seven years old, out on the jetty in the fog, too close to the water. A wave should have taken him — I saw it coming, I couldn't move. And then the sea set him down gentle as a mother, on dry rock. The ledger says GIVEN, but I knew what it was. A trade. The sea had taken your grandfather willingly, so it gave back what it could." He met her eyes. "Daniel's fifty-one now. He's first mate on the Meridian Star."

"Did you know?" Maya whispered. "When the collector named the ship — did you know your son was aboard?"

"I knew." Tomas's voice broke. "Forty years I've kept this harbour, and every night I look at that tower and I wonder whether the sea remembers the arithmetic. Whether it kept the change." He put his hand on her shoulder, and it was not cold now — it was shaking. "You write your name in that book, and you light that lamp tomorrow night, and whatever the sea owes my family, it pays it all at once. Do you understand me, girl? I'm not asking you to save three hundred strangers. I'm telling you my boy is on that ship."

Maya thought of the chalk word on the lamp housing. LIGHT. She thought of the ledger under her cheek, her own handwriting drying in the third column. "Then help me keep the crank."

They walked to the tower together, and the morning was grey and windless, and the great lens sat in its housing like a sleeping eye. Maya ran her hand over the chalk word and felt the iron cold beneath it. The oil reservoir was half full. The hand crank turned smooth under her palm — decades of palms had worn it so.

They oiled it and cleaned the lens with her mother's soft cloth, working without speaking.

Harrow came at noon, with two men and a cutting torch.

He was a thin man in a mainland coat, dry shoes picking their way up the tower steps with distaste. He looked at Maya the way men like him look at inconveniences. "Miss Rane. The Board requires the lamp room sealed and the mechanism disabled. This light is decommissioned. Any beam shown from Gullpoint will be treated as interference with lawful maritime commerce."

"Lawful," Maya repeated. She stood between him and the crank.

"The Meridian Star rounds the point tomorrow night under order 12-B." Harrow's smile was a clerk's smile. "The reef will do its work. The insurance will do its work. And you will be on the morning ferry with your cottage sold, if you are sensible."

Tomas stepped forward. "The girl's mother kept that light twenty-eight years."

"And her mother is dead." Harrow gestured to his men. "The crank."

One of the men reached for it. Maya put her hand on the iron housing — on the chalk word LIGHT — and said, "Touch it and I'll have you for trespass. This cottage is mine. The tower is mine. The Board decommissioned the light; it did not purchase my roof."

Harrow studied her. "You found the ledgers."

It was not a question.

"They're mine too," Maya said.

"Then you know what the sea does to keepers who break the terms." He leaned close, and his breath smelled of mainland coffee. "Your mother learned. Your father learned. Light that lamp tomorrow night, Miss Rane, and you'll find the Board's patience is shorter than the sea's. The sea may wait a hundred years. I won't wait a hundred minutes."

He left the torch unlit. But his men stayed on the causeway — two dark figures against the grey sand, watching the tower.

At dusk she climbed the stairs alone. The lamp room was cold. The lens was dark. On the glass, where the collector had pressed his hand two nights before, there was a fogged handprint that had not faded — and beneath it, written in condensation from the outside, in the sea's wet hand, a single word:

SEALED.

Maya looked at it a long time. The account was sealed — the price named, the bargain closed, the sea's side of it done. What remained was hers: the crank, the oil, the long night tomorrow, and two men on the causeway who would try to stop her, and three hundred souls she had never met, and one of them named Daniel, who had been seven years old and spared by the sea for a reason she was only now beginning to understand.

She put her palm against the cold iron of the crank. The chalk word was still there under her hand.

Tomorrow night, the Meridian Star would round the point. And Maya Rane — the next keeper, the last keeper — would be waiting at the top of the tower, with the lamp ready and the dark held off by nothing but her own two hands.
