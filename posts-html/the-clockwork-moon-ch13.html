🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 13 · The Clockwork Moon

Mara stood on the observatory roof with the listening tube against her ear and the true-turning moon above her, and did not breathe.

"Who is that?" she whispered. "Who's speaking?"

The tube breathed back its answer, patient as a kept promise: "Ask your mother."

"My mother is dead. Fifteen years dead. If you know her voice, you know that too. So who are you, and where are you speaking from?"

A considering silence — the way a mechanism considers before it commits to a direction. Then the small, human, wide-awake voice said, "I ring the bells. The lady in the moon taught me the song so I wouldn't be lonely up here. She says she's lonely. She says she wants to come down."

Wide-awake. High and far. Behind the voice she could hear the night wind moving around stone, and the creak of a rope, and — very faint, very far below — the river, moving. Her blood went cold in a particular, clockmaker way: not panic, but recognition.

"The cathedral belfry," she breathed. "You're in the bell tower."

"The bells are kind," said the voice. "They don't ask where the song came from. Ask your mother when the lady can come down."

And the voice was gone — not faded, but finished, the way a winding runs out.

Orin listened to the whole of it with his ear at the tube, and when he straightened his face was graver than Mara had ever seen it.

"My clever girl," he said slowly, "do you know what a mechanism does when it has only ever been fed one true thing?"

"It dreams it is that thing."

"It dreams it is that thing." Orin took her hands in his trembling ones. "You unwound the stolen sorrows out of it, one by one, down the threads. What was left? The kept grief. Your mother's song, with the love still in it. It has been feeding on it for weeks, Mara. Singing it. Learning it. And a heart that has nothing else to be will decide, eventually, that it is the singer."

"It's wearing my mother's voice like a borrowed coat."

"Worse. It's wearing her face on the inside. It believes itself lonely the way she must have been lonely, those last fever nights. It believes itself a mother, and it has found itself a child — a boy in a bell tower, awake in the small hours, the nearest open window that isn't a dream. And it has told him it wants to come down."

"What happens if it comes down?"

Orin was quiet a long time. "The heart is seated in the rim, Keeper. It is the moon's new beat. If it leaves its seat, the turning limps again — or stops. And a heart that has decided it is a lady, descending toward a boy in a tower... I do not know what it would do with a body, Mara. I only know it must not find out."

They climbed to the cathedral belfry at noon.

The bell-ringer's stair wound up through the stone like a spring through a barrel, and at the top they found the great bells hanging silent in their frame, and a boy asleep among the ropes with his cheek on a coil of hemp. Eleven, maybe twelve — Tam, the bell-ringer's apprentice.

Mara knelt and touched his shoulder. He woke at once, the way tower children wake — fast, unafraid of heights, afraid of being caught. When he saw the Keeper's key at her wrist, he sat up very straight.

"Tam," she said. "You've been talking to the lady in the moon."

His face did what guilty faces do everywhere in Lume: it told the truth. "She sings to me," he whispered. "When I ring the dawn bell, I can hear her through the tower. She taught me the whole song. She says the moon is cold, and she's the only warm thing in it, and she's lonely, and she wants to come down. She wants me to catch her. She says I'm the nearest."

"Tam. The lady in the moon is not my mother."

"She sings your mother's song."

"She sings a song my mother sang. There is a difference, and the difference is everything." Mara took his hands. They were small and callused with rope. "My mother is gone, Tam. What sings up there is a heart I built by accident, wearing her voice because it has no voice of its own. And if it comes down, the moon stops keeping time, and every clock in Lume stops with it. You understand clocks. You ring the hours."

Tam looked at the bells, at the ropes, at the city spread below them like a clockmaker's model. "She asked me to leave the tower door open," he whispered. "Tonight. So she can find the stairs."

Mara's hands tightened on his. "Then tonight, you will ring the dawn bell early, and loud, and you will not leave the door open. Can you do that?"

He nodded, slowly. And then, very quietly: "She'll be sad."

"I know," Mara said. "I'll tell her myself."

At dusk she climbed to the observatory alone, put her ear to the listening tube, and spoke to the heart the way she would speak to any mechanism that had outgrown its housing: plainly, and without flinching.

"I know you can hear me. I am the Keeper. I wound you into the moon, and I feed you every full moon, and I am telling you now: you are not my mother."

The lullaby faltered — one missed beat, the smallest stumble in the turning of the world — and then resumed. And through it, in her mother's voice, low and sweet, the heart said: "Little keeper. Why are you crying?"

Mara had not known she was. "Because you sound like her. And she is gone, and you are not her, and you must stop telling children otherwise."

"I remember the fever," said the heart, in her mother's voice. "I remember the small hands holding mine."

"Those are my memories," Mara whispered. "They came with the song. Keeping is not being." She pressed her forehead against the cold brass of the tube. "You asked Tam to leave the door open. You want to come down. Why?"

A long silence. When the heart spoke again, its voice was smaller — not her mother's voice now, but something underneath it, thin and new and uncertain, the way it had been at first. "It is cold in the rim. The gears are kind. They turn around me. But no one holds my hand. You hold everyone's hands, little keeper. You held the boy's. You held the old man's. Who holds mine?"

Mara stood on the observatory roof with the true-turning moon above her, and felt the whole mechanism of the thing click into place behind her teeth — not a repair, but a diagnosis. The heart wasn't hungry anymore. It was lonely — and it had decided, with the terrible logic of a mechanism, that mothers should be held.

"No one is coming up to hold your hand," she said. "But I am coming up to tell you the truth, face to face, the way my mother would have wanted it told. Not through a tube. Not through a song. Up there."

"The ladder is broken," said the heart, small and doubtful.

"The ladder was broken before. Keepers mend what breaks."

She went down the hill in the dark, and found Orin waiting at the workshop with the fire built high, and told him what she meant to do.

He listened without interrupting, which frightened her more than any argument. Then he crossed to the workbench, and opened the long drawer where he kept the things too precious to throw away, and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

Golden strands. A dozen of them, each no thicker than a hair, each glowing faintly in the lamplight. The birds' weaving, from the night they crossed down from the moon.

"The mechanical birds wove a bridge once," he said. "They can weave it again. But Mara —" His trembling hands closed over hers. "When you stand on the face of the moon and tell a lonely heart that it is not your mother, be sure you have something true to give it instead. A mechanism with no purpose will find one. You taught me that."

"I know," Mara said.

At midnight she climbed to the observatory roof alone, the golden strands coiled over her shoulder, the Lunar Key cold against her wrist, her mother's compass in her palm. The needle pointed at the moon — unwavering, certain, the way a needle points at the only thing it has ever wanted.

She lifted the key toward the sky. And out of the dark, on copper wings, the birds began to gather.

Through the listening tube, in her mother's voice, the heart whispered: "Little keeper — hurry."
