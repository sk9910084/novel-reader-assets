🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 5 · The Atlas of Rain

A year after the Tempest Crown, Iris Bell had a problem she had never imagined having. Too many people wanted her maps. The attic above Greywake harbor had become a place of pilgrimage. Captains arrived with charts creased from years in oilskin pockets, begging her to copy their secret fishing grounds with the open compass mark that had made her famous. Children came with maps of imaginary countries drawn in crayon, which Iris treated with exactly the same seriousness as the navigational charts, because she had learned that every coastline begins as an act of imagination.

Her father had taken to sitting in the corner of the attic with his mending, because the fishermen had finally taught him knots that held, and he watched the parade of visitors with an expression Iris realized was pride learning to sit quietly. He had planted a garden behind the house, and tended it with the same patience he had once practiced waiting for roots to grow.

The living atlas rested on the great table like a sleeping animal. Its rivers still moved when rain touched the roof, restless blue lines rearranging themselves into truths the world had not yet spoken aloud. Iris no longer treated it as an answer book. The Rainkeepers had asked it what to command, and been ruined. She asked it what was true, and it answered in ink.

On a grey Tuesday, exactly a year and a day after the storm had divided, the truth it showed her was terrible.

It began with the ship. A courier brig from the south limped into Greywake harbor with half its rigging gone and its hull scraped raw, as though it had been dragged along a reef by something impatient. The captain was a woman Iris did not know, with salt-bleached hair and a burn across her forearm shaped like a hand. In her cabin, seasick and furious about it, sat a girl no older than sixteen, an apprentice from Mael's open school at Pluvia, clutching a wax-sealed satchel like it was the only dry thing left in the world.

Her name was Wren. She had the ink-stained fingers of a true archivist and the exhausted look of someone who had sailed straight through weather to deliver a message.

"Archivist Mael sent me himself," Wren said, in the attic, rain drumming on the roof above them. "He said I was not to trust this letter to any ship whose captain I could not look in the eye. He said you would understand why."

She laid the satchel on the great table, and Iris's stomach tightened, because she recognized the weight of it. Letters with that kind of weight were never about anything small.

The rain on the roof grew louder, as though the weather itself were listening.

Mael's letter was short, in his precise archivist's hand, and Iris read it twice because the first time her eyes refused to believe it.

"The school has been studying the Rainkeepers' marginalia," he wrote, "the notes they scribbled in the borders of their official logs. Students found references, repeated across a dozen hands, to three instruments. Not one. Three. The Atlas of Rain, which remembers. The Tide Ledger, which records the debts of every storm ever steered, the price the weather paid. And the Wind Rose, a compass that points not toward north but toward the weather that has been moved, the rain taken from where it belonged."

Iris looked up. Wren was watching her with archivist's eyes, the kind that notice everything.

"Read the rest," Wren said quietly.

"The Tide Ledger was burned with the Guild's stronghold twenty years ago. That, at least, is ash. But the Wind Rose was never destroyed. A royal factor purchased it at a private sale three months past, in a Meridian port. The Meridian has founded a Royal Weather Office. They are offering rain to paying ports again, openly. They have installed a royal cartographer, a man named Vey, and students who have seen his charts say the coastlines move."

Tomas had gone very still. Iris felt the old cold settling into her bones, the same cold she had felt the morning the black coastline painted itself over Greywake.

"There is worse," Wren said, and opened the satchel's inner pocket, and drew out a folded page.

It was a chart fragment on the cheap paper the open school used for student work. It showed a chain of islands she knew better than her own hands: the Quiet Eye's islands, her father's living breakwater. But someone had drawn over them in red ink, the root bridges crossed out one by one. Beside each crossing, in a strange, angular hand, was a single word, repeated like a tolling bell.

"This was confiscated from a Meridian survey ship that put into Pluvia for repairs," Wren said. "The captain claimed it was a navigational exercise. The students copied it before the Meridian crew could take it back. None of us can read the word. But the school's linguists say it appears in old Guild marginalia, and that it translates roughly to unmaking."

Tomas stood up so suddenly his chair scraped the floorboards. He was staring at the page with an expression Iris had seen only once before, on the black sand of the largest island, when Mael's glider had dropped out of the cloud wall.

"They're going to cut the roots," he said.

Before Iris could answer, the atlas on the great table shivered.

She had felt it move a thousand times, the living pages restless as water. This was different. This was a shudder, like a horse feeling a rider it did not trust. The rivers on the open page slowed, thickened, and went still. And then, across the page, ink began to bloom.

It was not her father's hand. It was not her hand. It was the strange, angular hand from the confiscated chart, and it was drawing in the same moving blue as the atlas's own rivers, as though the book recognized this hand as one of its own.

The ink drew a coastline Iris had never seen, far to the south, where her charts showed only white space. It drew it carefully, deliberately, the way a thief draws a plan. And then it drew, in red, a fleet of small black ships, bearing the seal of the Meridian crown, sailing north toward a chain of islands connected by living green bridges.

Tomas's breath caught. Iris's did too.

The instruments were paired. That was what the marginalia meant. The atlas did not belong to her. It had never belonged to her. It belonged to the weather, and any hand holding one of the three instruments could write in it. She had been reading the truth in its pages for a year, and somewhere across the sea, a royal cartographer named Vey had been writing in the same ink.

The foreign ink finished its drawing and settled, gleaming wetly. Beneath it, in that angular hand, words bloomed like something dredged from deep water: "The roots will be severed. The storms will be free. The arithmetic begins again."

Wren made a small sound. Tomas's fists were clenched at his sides.

Iris stood very still, and she felt something rising in her that she had not felt in a year, not since the morning she had thrust the atlas at Captain Soren on the quay and demanded he sail into a storm. It was the mapmaker's fury, the cold clear anger of someone who has learned that a map is a promise, and who has just watched someone use a promise as a weapon.

She pressed her palm flat against the page, the way she had once pressed it against her father's two words, I am here. The foreign ink was warm. It pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat that was not hers.

"Then we go south," she said. "Before the fleet does."

Tomas looked at her, and she saw the seven years in his eyes, the islands, the cables, the grafting knives, all of it rising up behind his gaze. She saw him measure the garden, the mended nets, the quiet he had earned, against the roots he had grown with his own scarred hands.

He picked up his coat from the peg by the door.

"Get Soren," he said. "The Finch still has her new sails. And Iris." He paused, and his voice went rough. "This time we take the atlas's warning for what it is. Not a map of what will happen. A map of what someone wants us to believe will happen. There is a difference, and I think it matters."

Above them the rain hammered the roof, and in the living atlas the blue rivers began to move again, restless as ever, as if the weather itself were bracing for what was coming. On the page beside the foreign fleet, new ink was already blooming, faint and uncertain, in a hand Iris almost recognized.

It was her own.
