🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 8 · The Clockwork Moon

On the eve of the full moon, Mara climbed to the observatory and put her ear to the listening tube, and the moon sang to her.

It was not the slow geological turning she had listened to for two years. Beneath the great gears ran a choir — hundreds of harvested griefs humming in harmony through the silver threads, tuning themselves note by note, and the moon's own mechanism answering, tooth by silver tooth, the way an instrument answers a tuning fork. The astronomer stood beside her with his hands clasped behind his back, and his face was the color of old paper.

'It is preparing a place,' he said. 'The rim, where the old gear sat. The threads are drawing the cradle-tooth upward even now. By midnight tomorrow it will seat itself, and the mechanism will take it, the way a lock takes a key.'

'And then?'

'And then Lume will have its grief back,' the astronomer said, 'all at once, and wearing a crown.'

Orin was waiting at the workshop with the fire built high and his tools laid out in a row he had not touched, which told Mara everything about the state of his thoughts before he spoke a word.

'We cannot smash it,' he said. 'Break the cradle and every grief it holds will loose at once — a storm of sorrow with no key to gentle it. The last storm cracked the moon. This one would break the city.'

'We cannot buy the grief back,' Mara said. 'The threads steal it home. I watched Petra's wedding day rise like smoke.'

'So we must spoil it.' Orin leaned forward, the blanket slipping from his knees, his eyes bright with the terrible clarity that age had never dimmed. 'Think, my clever girl. What has the tooth eaten? Only grief that was thrown away — sold, abandoned, priced and discarded. Joy refuses to be property; it will not sit still in glass. But there is a grief the tooth has never tasted, and never will, unless you offer it.'

'What grief?'

'Kept grief.' He reached out and covered her hand with his trembling one. 'The lullaby you carried for fifteen years. The compass you would not repair. The mornings you woke reaching for her and got up and worked anyway. You did not throw your grief away, Mara. You held it, and it held you, and somewhere inside it the love stayed alive. The tooth has eaten ten thousand hollow griefs. It has never once tasted a grief with the love still in it.'

Mara looked at the iron key hanging above the door, at the rusted spring, at the empty nail where her mother's compass should have hung. 'You want me to feed it my mother.'

'I want you to sing to it,' Orin said. 'The whole song. Including the last line it taught you. Give it the one thing it cannot digest, and let us see what breaks first.'

They came to the river camp at dusk, and the camp had changed.

The wagons stood in their ring, but the fire had gone out, and above the cradle Varric had raised a frame of cable and brass, an openwork spire aimed at the darkening sky. The black tooth within the cradle had grown whole — great and matte and lightless, pulsing like a second heart — and it was straining upward against its lashings, trembling, eager. High above, the silver threads had descended lower than Mara had ever seen them, a trembling curtain of light reaching down through the dusk toward the camp, toward the tooth, the way roots reach for water.

'It doesn't need my ladder,' Varric said. He stood beside the cradle in his wire-thin gray, his hands resting on the brass as gently as a father over a sick child. The hollow people stood around the wagons in a silent ring, watching, their faces empty and hopeful at once. 'The moon is reaching for it, Keeper. It only needs one voice to call it home. Mine was never enough. It wants the voice it learned first.'

'Don't,' Cael said quietly, from behind Mara. His hand was on the wrench at his belt, and his face was the face of a boy of nineteen carrying a prince from a river. 'Mara, don't go near it.'

But Mara was already walking forward, the compass in one hand and nothing in the other, and the tooth's hum deepened as she came, shaped itself, became her mother's voice humming the lullaby's first line, inviting, certain, the way a door invites a key.

She stood before the cradle, and she sang.

She sang it the way her mother had sung it, low and sweet, the way she had sung it to herself in the dark for fifteen years. She sang the verses she had kept, and then — her voice steady, her eyes open, her hand resting unafraid on the cradle's brass rim — she sang the last line. The lost line. The line the tooth had taught her two nights before, given back now freely, carried home on a voice that had chosen to keep it.

The tooth shuddered.

A hairline crack split its matte black surface, thin as a hair, bright at the edges — and from the crack poured a single memory, clear as water over stone: a boy's laugh, bright and fearless, eleven years old, flying a paper kite in a spring wind, before the floods, before the bridge came too late.

Varric made a sound like a man struck in the chest. He tore his hands from the cradle and stumbled backward, and the lashings — the lashings he had tended with such terrible tenderness — slipped from his fingers and fell slack. 'No,' he whispered. 'No, that one was mine to keep, that one was —' He sank to his knees in the trampled grass, weeping, while his son's laugh rang once more through the camp and dissolved into the dusk like breath on cold air.

The hollow people stirred. One by one, they turned their faces up — not toward Varric, but toward the sky, where the threads blazed suddenly brighter, trembling, taut.

The moon took its tooth.

It rose from the cradle in a slow spiral of dark light, trailing threads like a comet's tail, and Mara felt it through the soles of her feet, a lurch and a wrongness she knew too well. Up it went, past the rooftops of Lume, past the observatory dome where the astronomer stood with his hands over his mouth, up to the great clock-face turning in its slow majesty. And it seated itself in the rim — not where the old black gear had sat, but beside it, in the place the mechanism had been preparing — with a sound like a key turning in a lock the size of the sky.

The moon shuddered. Every silver thread rang at once, a chord so pure it hurt — and then, beneath the chord, a new rhythm took hold of the gears. Syncopated. Off-beat. A limp in the turning of the world.

And on the face of the moon, beside the new black tooth, the old seam — the thin dark line Mara had called proof that broken things could mend — split open again. Thin as a hair. Dark as a held breath.

The moon had taken its tooth back. And it was still hungry.

Mara stood in the trampled grass of the river camp with her mother's lullaby dying on her lips, and Varric weeping at her feet, and Cael's hand gripping her shoulder hard enough to bruise. Slowly, she looked down at her mother's compass in her hand.

The needle no longer pointed at the sky.

It pointed at her. At her heart. Steady and certain, the way a needle points at the only thing it has ever wanted.

And it was not pointing north.
