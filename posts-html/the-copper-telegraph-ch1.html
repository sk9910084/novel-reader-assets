The sounder clicked at half past eleven, and Ruth Calloway stopped breathing.

It was not the hour that startled her. Telegraph offices in dying towns keep strange hours, and Red Cinder was dying faster than most. The railroad had chosen the northern pass three years back, and the town had been bleeding people ever since — first the assay office, then the schoolmistress, then half the street of clapboard houses that stood empty now with their shutters banging in the wind like slow applause. Ruth stayed because the office was hers and the office was Samuel's before it was hers, and she had nowhere else she cared to be.

It was not the hour. It was the direction the message came from.

Her sounder was wired to one line only: the Tucson trunk, running south through dust and creosote bush. The Copper Creek spur had been dead for twenty years. Everyone knew that. The poles still stood along the old mule trail, leaning like drunken fence posts, but the wire had been cut down and sold for scrap in the spring of 1870, six months after the fire.

The fire. Ruth's pencil hovered above the message pad.

In the autumn of 1869, the relay station at Copper Creek had burned to its foundations in a single night. Samuel had been the night operator there — twenty-six years old, quick-fingered, forever whistling between transmissions. The company report said a lantern tipped during a storm. The company report said no one could have survived. The company sent her a watch that had stopped at 2:14 and a letter signed by a man in San Francisco who had never met her husband.

She had buried the watch. She had kept the letter in a drawer she never opened.

Now the sounder clicked again — deliberate, rhythmic, no storm static in it at all — and Ruth found herself reaching for the key before her mind had finished objecting.

The header came through clean as spring water: COPPER CREEK RELAY.

Her hand shook so badly she nearly dropped the pencil. She forced herself to copy, letter by letter, the way Samuel had taught her on their wedding night, sitting knee to knee at this very desk with the lamp turned low.

RUTH STOP THEY LIED ABOUT THE FIRE STOP COME ALONE STOP SAMUEL

The office went very quiet. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters. Somewhere down the empty street, a dog barked once and thought better of it.

Ruth read the message four times. Then she did what any sensible widow of forty-one years would do: she stood up, walked to the window, and looked out at the line of poles marching south toward Tucson, their wires black against a bruised sky. Then she turned and looked at the corner of the room where the old spur jack still sat in the wall, dust-thick, its brass gone green — disconnected since before her daughter was born. Her daughter, grown and married now, living in Prescott with two boys Ruth saw at Christmas.

She sat back down. She checked the patch panel twice. Tucson line only. Copper Creek jack dark and dead as a pulled tooth.

"Storm charge," she said aloud, and her voice sounded thin in the empty room. "Induction. The wind does things."

But the wind does not know your name, and it does not know how to lie the way men lie — THEY LIED ABOUT THE FIRE — and it does not sign a dead man's name with the particular lazy spacing Samuel had always put after the letter L, a habit she'd teased him about a hundred times.

Before she let herself think any further, Ruth did the professional thing — the thing Samuel would have done. She patched through to Tucson on the live trunk and sent the test call, quick and clean: RED CINDER TESTING STOP ANY TRAFFIC TONIGHT STOP

The reply came back inside a minute, the Tucson night man unhurried: TUCSON HERE STOP QUIET NIGHT STOP ONLY YOU AND THE WIND STOP

She sat with that for a while. Only her and the wind. The Tucson man had no reason to play games; he was sixty if he was a day and half deaf, and he had never once, in eleven years of shared nights, sent her anything but the weather and the train times.

She considered the prank theory the way you consider a scorpion in your boot — carefully, and at a distance. Who in Red Cinder knew the Copper Creek call sign? Old Hobart at the livery, maybe, who had driven the mail run back when there was mail. The railroad surveyors, long gone. The company men in San Francisco who signed letters without meeting anyone.

None of them knew about the L. The lazy spacing after the letter L — that had been Samuel's private signature, a flaw in his fist, the kind of thing only a wife notices, the kind of thing no company manual ever recorded.

Ruth got up and made coffee she did not want, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. While the pot hissed, she stood at the map on the wall — the whole territory in faded ink, the live lines in red, the dead ones crossed out in black. Copper Creek sat eleven miles northwest, a black X through it, and beside the X, in Samuel's own hand from a happier year, a small penciled heart she had never had the strength to erase.

Eleven miles. In daylight, a half day's ride. At night, in this wind, with the trail gone to gullies — madness.

Come alone, the message had said. Not come at dawn. Not come with the sheriff. Come alone.

She pulled the company report from the drawer she never opened. She had memorized it anyway. Lantern tipped during a storm. No survivors. Stopped watch, 2:14.

What the report had never explained — what she had asked about in three letters, none of them answered — was why a night operator would have a lantern burning at his desk at all, when the relay station had been wired for electric light the year before. Samuel had written to her about it, proud as a boy: no more oil smoke on the message pads, he'd said. No more burnt fingers.

A lantern. In a station with electric light.

Ruth folded the report and put it back. Her hands had stopped shaking. That frightened her more than the shaking had.

She went to the door and bolted it — a ridiculous gesture, bolting a door against a ghost — and returned to the desk. She put her hand on the key. Copper Creek was eleven miles up the mule trail, through country that had gone back to coyotes and rattlesnakes. She had not been there in twenty years. She had never gone back. There had been nothing to go back to but a stone foundation and a brass plaque with his name spelled wrong — SAMUAL, the company stonecutter's error, which had always struck her as the final insult.

Her finger rested on the brass key for a long moment. Everything she knew about the world — about wire and weather, about the dead staying dead — stood on one side of that key. On the other side stood a spacing after the letter L that no one else alive could have faked.

She thought of the plaque with his name spelled wrong. SAMUAL. Twenty years of visiting a stone that couldn't even get his name right, and all the while the truth — if this was truth — sitting eleven miles up a dead trail, waiting for her to finally come and ask.

She tapped out, on the dead spur jack, the way you knock on a coffin lid just to be sure: RUTH HERE STOP WHO IS THIS STOP

The sounder answered before her finger left the key.
