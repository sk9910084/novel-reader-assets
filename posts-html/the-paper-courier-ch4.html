🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 4 · The Paper Courier

Elias walked home from the pier with the photograph in his breast pocket and the fog closing behind him like a page turned. He did not go to Margaret Hale's. The instruction burned in him — tell no one, not even the mother — but it was the photograph that kept his feet on his own street. He could not knock on a woman's door carrying her son's face and then carry it away again. A proof, once pulled, cannot be unseen; he knew that better than anyone. He would give it to her after Tuesday, or burn it, as the boy had said, and either way he would be the one who had set the type.

He slept badly and woke before the light, with the docket book open on the kitchen table and the September entry staring up like an accusation in his own young hand. The next issue bound on Tuesday — Thomas had said so — and the Office did not run on the Gazette's clock. A pledge was a small thing, in the trade: set it, pull a proof, read it, correct it. Nothing was final until the stone was locked. He had Monday.

He spent Monday morning at the archive because routine was the only alibi he had left, smiling at the night girl while he fed volumes into the scanner. By noon he had decided. The offer was Thomas's word for it; the ledger would be his own. But a printer does not buy a forme he has not examined. He would look at the man who had set forty-one notices and his own, and then he would decide what Tuesday meant.

The chandlery stood on the harborside row, smelling of tar and salt even through the door. Elias knew the owner, a broad man named Daniel Cobb, and asked after the rooms above, casual as a man asking about the weather — and Cobb's face did a small closing, like a shutter coming down. "Rented," Cobb said. "A gentleman. Keeps to himself. Two years, come spring. Pays in cash, through a solicitor in the city."

"Why do you ask, Elias?"

"The Gazette owes him a delivery correction." It sat on Elias's tongue like a bad sort. "Stairs at the back?"

Cobb nodded slowly, the way a man nods when he has decided not to argue with the weather. "Third step creaks."

The stairwell smelled of damp wool and paraffin. At the top, a landing, one door, and a smell Elias knew before he knocked: camphor and unwashed linen, with underneath it the smell of hot metal and ink. A printer's smell, in a room where no press could be. He knocked, and when nothing answered he tried the handle, and the door opened, because some doors in this town are only ever locked against the people who have not yet been summoned.

The room was small and clean in the way of rooms kept by strangers: a narrow bed, a chair, a paraffin stove ticking, a shelf of books bound in black cloth, and on the table by the window a hand press — a tabletop platen of the kind the Gazette had pensioned off in '89, its flywheel oiled and gleaming. Beside it, a composing stick. Beside that, a single galley tray with set type under a dust cloth.

In the bed lay an old man, breathing shallow enough to be mistaken for stillness. Eighty, perhaps more, his face the color of old paper, his hands folded on the blanket with the crooked knuckles of a man who had held a composing stick for sixty years. Elias knew those hands the way he knew his own. He did not mean to wake him — but the old man's eyes opened, grey and clear as water over type, and fixed on him without surprise.

"Vane," the old man said. "You've come up the stairs. I told the boy you would."

"You're the printer."

"I was the printer." The old man gestured at the chair, and Elias sat, because printers sit when another printer offers a chair; it is the oldest courtesy in the trade. "My name is Silas Crowe. You won't find me in your archive, and that is the point of me."

"Forty-one notices."

"Forty-two. The boy counts, even if the bindery hasn't bound him yet. And the forty-third is set. I set it myself, with these hands, and I am sorry for it, and I would do it again."

Elias sat. "Thomas says the notices are a ledger. Withdrawals. People who went."

"Thomas says what he was taught to say, and what he was taught is true the way a headline is true — it leaves out the body copy. Some went, yes. Most. But some were helped to go, Elias. And one or two did not go at all."

The green satchel. Elias heard himself say it before he knew he was going to speak. "The satchel. Burned unopened, for the insurance."

Crowe closed his eyes. "A man's whole name was in that satchel. Letters. A passport that wasn't his. When he died — a natural death, a heart in a boarding house in the city — the Office decided his paper life was a risk. So I set the notice, and the ferry office kept the satchel a year, and then they burned it, and I have carried the smell of that burning for twenty-three years. I told myself paper burns clean." A pause. "It doesn't. Nothing burns clean."

Elias thought of Lena's laugh, and of the line in the chase — His wife is dead and asks after him — and the anger came back, slow and hot. "You used her name."

"I know. It is the one thing in forty years I would take back if the trade allowed take-backs. It doesn't." Crowe's hand moved on the blanket, a small restless setting of invisible type. "That's why I asked for you by name, Elias. Because you found the seam. Forty years, and no one found it, and then a retired printer with a scanner found it in three weeks."

"That the ledger needs a printer."

"That the ledger needs a reader. The new men — the ones who pay Cobb in cash through a city solicitor — they print, but they don't read. They'll set whatever they're told and never ask what the white space says. You read the white space. You saw the notices were instructions, then the ledger behind them, then the man behind the ledger, and you came up the stairs. Nobody has come up the stairs in two years."

"Tuesday," Elias said. "They'll want my answer Tuesday."

"Say yes, and you print the notices, and you keep the true dockets, and boys like Thomas get to go on living. Say no — " He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Elias had read the white space.

"What would you do?" Elias asked. "If you were me. With the photograph in your pocket."

Crowe smiled, a terrible, paper-thin smile. "I'd burn the photograph, Elias. Not because of the boy. Because of the mother. Hope is a heavier thing to carry than grief, and she's carried grief this long." The smile faded. "But I am not you. I am the man who burned the satchel. Don't take counsel from me. Take it from the type."

The fog-horn sounded, once, for the incoming ferry. Crowe's eyes closed again, and Elias understood he was dismissed — not unkindly, the way a forme is dismissed from the stone when the run is done. He stood, and at the door he stopped.

"Silas. Is Thomas safe? Truly?"

A long silence. Then: "The boy is in the ledger, Elias. Nobody in the ledger is safe. That is what the ledger is for."

He went down the stairs and out past Cobb's counter with a nod he hoped looked ordinary, into a fog that had thickened while he was upstairs, as if the town had been holding its breath. He walked home the long way, past Margaret Hale's street, and did not turn down it, and the photograph burned in his pocket like a coal.

At home he laid the slip from the pier, the photograph, and the docket book on the kitchen table in a row — three proofs — and he read them the way he had been trained to read: proof it. Check every line. The slip said the pledge was due Tuesday. The photograph said the gulls are good here. The docket book, in his own young hand, said a notice had been set from copy received, and paid from an account that did not exist.

He sat at the table as the light failed and the fog pressed at the glass, thinking about paper, and fire, and the weight of a chase, and about what the white space between two futures was trying to tell him.

The fog-horn sounded twice.

There was no ferry due.
