Maya Rane had not come home to grieve. She had come to sell the cottage, empty the lighthouse of its forty years of salt and silence, and be back on the mainland before the weekend ferry. That was the plan she had repeated to herself on the boat, the plan written in her diary in the neat hand of someone who believed plans mattered.

The Gullpoint light stood at the end of the causeway, white paint gone grey at the edges, its lamp room dark. Her mother had kept the light for twenty-eight years, through storms that tore roofs off the harbour town and winters when the causeway disappeared under the sea. When the letter came — a polite, printed condolence from the Lighthouse Board, the light officially decommissioned, the cottage hers to dispose of — Maya had felt nothing. She had been nine the last time she visited. She was thirty-four now. The grief, such as it was, had been spent long ago, in smaller currencies: missed birthdays, unanswered calls, the long static of a mother who loved a lamp more than her daughter.

The cottage smelled of damp paper and kerosene. Everything was where it had always been. The kitchen table with the one burned ring from the kettle. The brass clock that had stopped at 4:17, as it had been stopped since Maya was a child, because her mother said a clock that told time would only remind her of the ships she could not save.

Maya worked methodically. Clothes into bags for the charity shop. The good copper pans to keep. Her mother's letters, bundled with string, which she put aside without opening. That morning she had gone first to the harbour office to sign the decommission papers. Old Tomas, the harbormaster, had stamped the forms without looking up — and then, as she turned to leave, he had caught her wrist. His hand was cold.

"Your mother," he had said, "was a good keeper. But ask around town, girl. Ask about the dark nights."

"What dark nights?"

"The nights the light went out." He let go of her wrist. "Thirty years I've kept this harbour. I know every wreck on that reef by name. And I know which ones happened on nights your mother's lamp was burning — and which ones happened on nights it wasn't." He pushed the papers toward her. "Sign, take the cottage, sell it. Don't dig. Some things on Gullpoint are buried for a reason."

She had signed and left, annoyed at the old man's riddles. She thought of his words now, standing over the open hatch.

She had just finished the bedroom when she found the door.

It was under the rug in the sitting room — a square of darker wood, a hatch with an iron ring. Maya would have walked over it her whole childhood without knowing. She knelt and pulled.

A ladder went down into a root cellar no bigger than a pantry, dry and smelling of wax. And in the cellar were shelves. Shelves of ledgers.

There were thirty-one of them, bound in dark cloth, each one lettered on the spine in her mother's careful hand: a year, and beneath it a word. Maya lifted the first one. 1962 — KEPT. Then 1963 — KEPT. She opened it.

It was not a log of ships guided home. It was the opposite.

Each page held a date, a ship's name, a course, a time — and beside each, in her mother's hand: DARK. Kept dark. Board order 12-B. Light extinguished 22:40–23:10. Beside one entry, a note: two boats lost. Beside another: no survivors.

A memory surfaced, sharp as a splinter. She had been seven, visiting for the summer, and a storm had come in the night. She had woken to darkness — the lamp, which always turned above her bedroom ceiling, was still. She had crept up the tower stairs and found her mother sitting on the lamp-room floor with her back against the great lens, her face wet, whispering, "Not tonight. Not this one. Forgive me." Maya had never asked what it meant. Children accept strange things in their parents.

And the clock. The brass clock in the kitchen, stopped at 4:17 for thirty years. She understood now, with a sick lurch, that it had not broken. It had been stopped. Deliberately. A memorial to a minute.

Maya sat down hard on the cellar floor. This made no sense. A lighthouse's entire purpose was the light. Why would a keeper ever keep it dark — and worse, why would she record it, year after year, as if the books were a confession?

She pulled 1974 — FORBIDDEN. 1981 — FORBIDDEN. 1996 — COLLECTED. The last word chilled her. She opened it to the final page. The entries ran to October, three months ago — one month before her mother died. And then, on the last page, in ink so fresh it still shone, a single new entry. No date. No ship.

Only her own name.

MAYA RANE — the next keeper.

Her hands were shaking. She told herself this was grief, delayed and misaddressed, the theatrical nonsense of an old woman who had spent too long alone with a lamp. But the ink was wet. Her mother had been dead five weeks.

Then she heard the knock at the cottage door.

Maya froze. The ferry was not due until morning. The causeway was under water at this hour — she had checked the tide tables herself. Nobody could have walked in.

The knock came again. Three times. Slow. Deliberate.

She climbed the ladder with the ledger still in her hands and crossed the sitting room. Through the salt-caked window she could see the figure on the step: a man in a black oilskin coat, water running off him in sheets, though the sky outside was clear and starry.

He was not wet from rain. He was wet from the sea.

"You're the keeper's daughter," he said, when she opened the door. It was not a question. His eyes were the colour of deep water, and he did not look at the ledger in her hands — but his glance flickered to it, once, the way a man flickers to a loaded gun.

"My mother is dead," Maya said.

"I know," the man said. "The sea sent me to collect."

Maya's fingers tightened on the ledger. "Collect what?"

"Her debt," he said. "Twenty-eight years of kept darkness. Every ship she let the reef take was a coin borrowed, and the sea keeps accounts better than any lighthouse board." He looked past her, into the cottage, at the open hatch in the floor. "But debts can be transferred. You read the last page, didn't you?"

"You stay away from me," Maya said, and tried to close the door.

He put his hand on it — his sleeve dripped seawater onto her mother's rug — and said, very quietly, "Three nights from now, the Meridian Star rounds the point. Big ship. Three hundred souls. Your mother's ledger says the light stays dark." He smiled, and his teeth were white as broken shell. "Unless the new keeper wishes to renegotiate."

And from the lamp room above them — the decommissioned lamp, dark for five weeks — a light began to turn.

Maya looked up. The great lens was moving, slow and stately, sweeping its beam across the black water. There was no power to the lighthouse. The Board had cut the cable herself — she had watched the work boat do it, that morning.

The man followed her gaze. "She's still keeping," he said softly. "Even now. Some debts don't end with death, keeper's daughter. They only change hands."

He stepped back into the dark, and the sea took him — not walked, not swam, but the dark water rose where he stood and he was simply gone, the way a reflection is gone when you look away.

Maya stood in the doorway with the ledger against her chest, seawater spreading dark across her mother's rug, and watched the lamp turn above the empty cottage. The beam swept out to sea, found nothing, swept back. Out. Back. Patient as a heartbeat.

On the last page of the ledger, beneath her name, words were appearing in wet ink, writing themselves as she watched:

THREE NIGHTS. THE MERIDIAN STAR. CHOOSE.
