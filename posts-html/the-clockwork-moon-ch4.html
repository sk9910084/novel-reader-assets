The mechanical bird came back at the edge of dawn. Mara heard its copper wings before she saw it, a bright metallic flutter against the grinding of the moon's closing shutters, and behind it came a flock of smaller birds—sparrows and swifts of hammered brass and tin, dozens of them, each carrying in its beak one loose strand of the fallen golden ladder. They wove the strands together in the air with impossible dexterity, beak to beak, strand over strand, until a bridge of braided gold hung between the moon's closing face and the observatory dome below. "Quickly," said the bird in Orin's voice, though Orin himself stood beside Mara, leaning on Cael's shoulder and smiling. "It won't hold the shutters long."

They crossed above a city waking from an endless midnight. Beneath their feet Lume stirred: lanterns guttering, shutters opening, voices rising in wonder and confusion. The river moved—Mara felt it in her bones before she saw it, that first shivering ripple breaking the silver stillness. When their feet touched the observatory roof, the eastern sky blushed, and then sunrise spilled over Lume like water over a dam: gold on the rooftops, gold on the river, gold on ten thousand upturned faces. People wept in the streets and did not know why. After a night that would not end, the morning felt like a pardon.

The clocks did not agree at first. Across the city they rang at different times—three o'clock from the cathedral, noon from the harbor, midnight from Flour Row—as the bottled moments came home. The Queen's stolen seconds were returning, unpredictably, exactly as Orin had warned, and Lume spent the morning in beautiful confusion. Old Fen, the grocer, stopped in the middle of weighing flour and sang three perfect bars of a lullaby he had never learned, then stared at his own hands in amazement; somewhere, a mother who had lost that song years ago found she could hum it again. Widow Lark, hanging her washing, suddenly laughed with her husband's laugh—his exact bright bark of a laugh—for one shining second, and then stood very still among the damp sheets, smiling through tears, because it had been ten years and she had forgotten the sound and now she remembered. Two strangers on the Marrow Lane bridge—no, on the street that was Marrow Lane again, restored with the returning time—caught each other's eye as the shared memory of a summer rain passed through them both, rain on hot cobbles, the smell of it, and by noon they were friends, sitting together at the tea house that had also returned, unable to explain why they felt they had known each other for years. And in a narrow house on Tanners' Row, an old man who had quarreled with his brother twenty years before woke weeping, because the bottled years had returned his brother's voice saying the forgiving words he had never lived to say, and the old man dressed himself and walked across the city to the brother's grave and spoke the answer aloud, and walked home lighter, as if a debt had been settled by kinder accountants than time.

The Queen dismantled the reversal machine publicly, in the palace courtyard, with the whole city watching. She stood before the tower-sized watch in her plain gray dress, the portrait chain conspicuously absent from her neck, and spoke without notes and without excuses. "I stopped the moon," she said, and the crowd's murmur died. "I stole your moments—your laughter, your grief, your rain—to feed my own sorrow. I told myself it was love. It was theft." She raised a sledgehammer herself and struck the first blow against the machine's brass face, and the engineers, after a hesitation, joined her with their tools. By evening the great watch was scrap, its cables coiled like dead snakes. The Queen opened the palace stores—grain, timber, coin—and set them flowing into the neighborhoods damaged when the moon slipped backward, and she did not call it generosity. She called it debt.

Cael Arden resigned his captaincy the same week. He laid his sword and his insignia on the Queen's desk and asked, instead, for the bridge commission. "I want to build the thing I failed," he told Mara when she found him at the riverbank, sleeves rolled, studying the plans. And he built it differently than any bridge in Lume's history: every warning the old bridge-master had ever given—about supports, about floods, about parade-day crowds—was carved into the plans in Cael's own hand, and then carved into the bridge itself, into the stone of the parapets, where every citizen could read them. Children traced the letters with their fingers on their way to school. "So no one has to trust my memory," he said. "The stone remembers."

Orin recovered slowly. The days the pendulum had taken from him did not return; his hair stayed white, and his hands never quite stopped trembling, though his spirit never wavered. But he returned to the workshop, and to his bench, and to his endless arguments with the astronomer, and he told Mara that age mattered less once one stopped treating hours as property. "I spent my life measuring time," he said one evening, watching her true a difficult escapement, "and it took being robbed of it to learn that time isn't a thing we own. It's a thing we keep—for each other."

Mara became the Keeper of the Lunar Key. The Queen offered her a palace title, lands, a seat on the council; Mara refused them all, politely and completely. Instead she hung the iron key above the ordinary workshop door, on a plain nail, between a rusted spring and her mother's dented compass—which, she noticed with a quiet start, had begun keeping true time again. Once each month, on the night of the full moon, she climbed to the observatory, as she had climbed that first night, and listened through the great telescope for irregular gears. The moon bore a thin dark line across its face where the grief had cracked it, visible on clear nights. People called it a flaw. Mara called it a seam: proof that broken things could be repaired without pretending they had never broken.

On the first anniversary of the long night, something extraordinary happened, and no one had ordered it. Word had spread quietly for weeks—no proclamation, only neighbors telling neighbors—and by midnight the great square before the palace was full, thousands of citizens standing shoulder to shoulder in the cold, holding lanterns. Mara stood in the workshop doorway with Orin's hand on her shoulder, and Cael beside them with his bridge-plans under his arm, and the Queen, unannounced and unguarded, somewhere in the crowd in her gray dress. At midnight, every clock in Lume—cathedral and harbor, workshop and kitchen, the great regulator and the smallest pocket watch—stopped, voluntarily, for one full minute. In that minute the whole city stood still together, remembering: the stopped night, the golden ladder, the things taken and returned. A child asked her father why everyone was quiet, and the father whispered, "Because we're listening to the hour." Then, as one, the clocks began again: a hundred voices striking the hour in imperfect, joyful unison, and the square erupted in cheers that rolled across the rooftops like sunrise. Then, as one, they began again: a hundred voices striking the hour in imperfect, joyful unison. Mara stood in the workshop doorway with Orin's hand on her shoulder and listened, and she thought of ladders and keys and birds, of grief made solid and unmade again, of a Queen's debt and a captain's bridge and a grocer's borrowed song, of her mother's lullaby returned to its rightful keeper. The new hour belonged to no king, no clockmaker, and no past. It belonged to everyone willing to meet it.
