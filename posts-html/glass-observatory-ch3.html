🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 3 · The Glass Observatory

Mara owned a car she almost never drove, a pre-sale hatchback with a cracked windshield and a radio that only received the Consortium's weather band. She loaded it at four in the afternoon with the things a person takes when she is not sure she is coming back: the logbook, the folded pages of ones and zeroes, a thermos, the star atlas, and, after a moment's hesitation, the cheap kitchen knife.

The coordinates pointed to a place the city pretended was not there. Forty kilometers north, past the last commuter rail stop, past the billboard farms where the lattice antennas rose like white trees. The scrubland began where the maintenance roads ended. Mara drove until the hatchback's navigation gave up and showed her a gray grid, and then she drove by the old method, the one she had learned from her professors: triangulate. She took three compass bearings off landmarks — a water tower, a relay mast, the pale smudge of the Observatory itself, twenty kilometers of haze to the south — and walked the last half kilometer with her phone's compass in one hand and the knife in her pocket.

She found the hut by its shadow.

It was small and wrong in the way that only deliberate things are wrong: four walls of corrugated composite, a roof weighted with stones, no windows, and on top of it a dish — not a commercial dish, not one of the flat phased-array panels the Consortium licensed, but an old parabolic dish, hand-aimed, pointed not at the horizon where the satellites lived but straight up, at the zenith, at the patch of sky the Consortium had auctioned last.

Mara stood in the scrub grass and looked at the dish for a long time. A dish pointed at the zenith, in an empty field, forty kilometers north of the city. A receiver, not a transmitter. Someone had been catching the heartbeat.

The door was unlocked. Inside, the hut smelled of paraffin and old paper, and it was full of notebooks.

They were stacked on a plank shelf that ran the length of one wall, dozens of them, the cheap kind with the marbled covers that libraries used to give away. Mara pulled one down at random and opened it. Page after page of ones and zeroes, in pencil, one column, the hand cramped and hurried. She pulled down another. The same. She checked the dates on the inside covers — the handwriting changed between notebooks, steadied, aged — and did the arithmetic the way she always did, reflexively, the way some people prayed.

Eleven years of notebooks. The earliest dated to the spring the sky auctions had closed.

Mara sat down on the crate that served as a chair and held the first notebook in her lap, and for a minute she could not read, because the scale of it was pressing on her chest like altitude. She had caught ninety seconds of the signal, through a basement light well, and she had been proud of her 1,847 bits. Whoever had sat in this hut had caught every maintenance window for eleven years. Tens of millions of bits. The whole conversation, or whatever it was, entire.

She worked through the shelf methodically, newest to oldest. The middle notebooks were the densest, the handwriting tight and furious. The recent ones were sparser. That interested her — she frowned at it, the way she frowned at anomalous photometer readings. The signal had been getting shorter. Or the transcriber had been getting tired. She could not tell which, and the distinction mattered, and there was no one to ask, because the crate was empty and the paraffin lamp was cold and there was a mug on the shelf with a ring of dried tea in the bottom of it and a thin layer of dust on everything except the dish outside, which was clean.

Someone maintained the dish. Someone had been here recently. Someone had stopped writing, but not stopped listening.

The newest notebook was on the shelf's end, its cover warped. Mara opened it. The last dated entry was three nights ago. And in the margin of the final page, in a different pencil — darker, pressed harder, the hand of someone in a hurry — someone had drawn a pattern Mara recognized the way she recognized her own face.

The heartbeat. Two on, one off, two on, three off.

Except it wasn't. She counted the marks three times, the way she had counted the bits in her kitchen. There was an extra beat at the end. Two on. One off. Two on. Three off. One on.

The loop marker had changed.

Mara stood up so fast the crate tipped over. She understood, all at once, with the clean terrible clarity of a calibration snapping into focus, what the seven-bit heartbeat had been. A loop marker. A start-here. And a loop marker only changes when the message changes — when whatever was broadcasting has something new to say, it announces it first, the way a lighthouse changes its flash pattern to warn of a different reef.

She looked at the date on the entry again. Three nights ago. The sky had said something new three nights ago, and the only person on Earth who knew — the person who had sat in this hut for eleven years — had stopped writing.

She searched the hut then, properly, the way she wished she had searched it first: under the shelf, behind the crate, in the tea tin, in the paraffin lamp's base. In the lamp's base she found a key she did not understand and a photograph she did not want to understand: a woman about Mara's age, standing in front of this hut, holding one of the notebooks up to the camera like a trophy. On the back, in the hurried dark pencil: "If you're reading this, it's your window now."

Mara put the photograph back exactly where she had found it. Her hands were shaking again. She told herself it was the drive, the heat, the altitude of the scrubland. She did not believe herself.

Outside, the dish pointed at the zenith, clean and waiting. The sun was dropping toward the billboard farms, and the first advertising swarms were rising for the evening shift, their lights winking on in the east like a rash. Mara calculated. It was 6:40. Her dome shift started at eleven. The maintenance window opened at 3:12.

She could do both. She could drive back, log light pollution with a steady hand, nod at the liaison's sealed-envelope smile, and be back at the hut by 3:00 with the survey scope from the basement — no, she couldn't move the scope, but she didn't need it. She needed her eyes, her stopwatch, and the dark pencil. The hut's owner had listened for eleven years with less.

She stood in the scrub grass and looked up at the daytime sky, the color of old television static, and for the first time in six years of night watches she felt the size of what she did not know pressing down like weather.

Two on. One off. Two on. Three off. One on.

Something up there had changed its mind. Something down here had stopped writing. And Mara Ellison, night-watch astronomer, keeper of a basement telescope no one knew about, was the only person on Earth standing in the exact spot where the two facts met.

She thought about the woman in the photograph — about Mara's age, holding a notebook up like a trophy. Eleven years of windows. Eleven years of dictation from the sky, and then, three nights ago, a changed heartbeat and silence. Whatever the woman had heard in that new message, she had not written it down. She had only hidden the key, left the note, and gone.

She locked the hut's door behind her — unlocked it again, because locking it felt like lying — and drove south toward the city with the photograph burning a hole in her memory and the new heartbeat counting itself over and over in her head, patient as a held breath, waiting for the window.
