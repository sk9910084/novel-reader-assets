🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 10 · The Clockwork Moon

The moon waited for her answer.

Mara stood on the brass frame of the empty cradle with the Lunar Key in her fist and her mother's compass on the rim, its needle fixed on the key with terrible devotion. Above her the great clock-face moon turned with its limp — one-two, one — two — and the black tooth pulsed in the rim like a wound that had learned to beat. Beside it, the old seam had opened like an eye. Something inside the moon was looking back at her through the widening dark, listening through the threads, learning the difference between grief thrown away and grief kept. It had been a boy flying a kite. A widow's last argument. A hundred borrowed mornings. It was becoming, tooth by tooth, a heart.

And it was asking her for the key.

She could hear the guards' torches on the river road, coming up through the dark like a second, smaller moonrise. The Queen's captain had promised her until the moon stood highest. The moon stood highest. Midnight was here.

"Mara," Cael whispered. His hand found hers, cold and certain. "What does it want with the key?"

Mara looked at the key in her palm. Dark iron. Intricately toothed. Warm as bread. The key that could wind backward or set free. "It doesn't want me to give it the key," she said slowly. "It wants me to use it. It wants to be wound backward. To before."

Orin stared at her from the fire's edge, his blanket slipping from his knees. "Mara —"

"The tooth feeds on thrown-away grief," she said, and her voice was steady now, the way it got when a mechanism finally showed her its logic. "On grief that's been priced and sold and poured out like smoke. Two years it's been eating hollow things. And then it tasted one true thing — my mother's song, kept fifteen years, the love still in it — and now it knows what it's missing." She lifted the key toward the sky. The silver threads trembled. They leaned toward the key the way roots lean toward water, the way the compass needle leaned toward her heart. "It can't un-eat what it ate. But I can wind it back. Thread by thread. Grief by grief. Back down the threads, back into the hearts they were taken from."

"You can't un-sell Petra's wedding day," Cael said. "It's in the tooth. It's in the moon."

Mara looked at Petra, standing at the edge of the hollow ring with tears on her face, singing the workshop song with the others. She thought of the bulb guttering on the wash-house table. She thought of keeping, which was a thing you did every morning with your own hands.

"Watch me," she said.

She raised the Lunar Key high, and the threads came to it.

They came the way they had always come to grief — drawn, hungry, certain — a hundred silver hairs detaching from the sky and spiraling down to wrap the key in light. Mara felt the moon's mechanism take hold through the iron in her fist, felt the great gears engage across nine miles of night the way she felt a train engage through a screwdriver: a click, deep as a bell, that traveled up her arm and settled behind her teeth.

And she turned the key backward.

The click was small. The effect was not.

Above them, the moon shuddered — and reversed. The great clock-face, which had turned one way for two years with its terrible limp, caught, hesitated, and began to turn the other way, one — two, one-two, the limp running backward like a film rewound. The black tooth in the rim flared, pulsed, and began — not to crack, not to shatter — to unwind.

Mara had expected it to fight. Instead it yielded the way a spring yields when you find its true direction: willingly, almost gratefully. The tooth's matte black surface loosened, thread by thread, and the first grief came down.

It came as dark mist wrapped in silver light, sliding down a thread like rain down a wire, and it found Widow Lark where she stood in the hollow ring — and the widow gasped, and her knees buckled, and she caught herself on Petra's shoulder, and she wept. The morning her husband died was hers again, all of it, the cold bedroom and the unmade tea and the last thing she'd never said — and it weighed more than an anvil, and it was hers.

Another click backward. Another thread dove. Old Fen took his burned winter back into his chest like a man catching a falling child, and sank to the grass, laughing and sobbing at once.

Click. Click. The moon turned backward above them, and the griefs rained down, and the hollow people filled — not with joy, not with light, but with the true weight of their own lives, sorrow by sorrow, and one by one they stopped singing the workshop song because their throats were too full, and stood in the trampled grass holding their griefs .

Petra's wedding day came down last of the great ones. Mara watched it descend — a grief the size of a held breath, silver-wrapped — and watched Petra lift her face to meet it, and watched the laundress take her whole lost day back into herself and stand swaying under it, tears running down her face, smiling the smile of a woman who has been given back something she was told she didn't need.

"It's heavier," Petra whispered, across the grass, to no one and to Mara. "It's heavier than the joy was. Keeper — it's heavier, and I wouldn't trade it back."

The tooth was shrinking. It was the size of a fist now, the size of a walnut, pulsing weaker, and the eye-seam beside it was narrowing, closing — and from inside the moon, through the closing dark, came a voice.

Her mother's voice.

"Keeper," it said. "Don't. Please. Don't let me go."

Mara's hand faltered on the key.

It was the lullaby's voice, low and sweet, the voice that had sung her to sleep for the first fifteen years of her life and then gone silent forever. It was the voice the tooth had learned, the voice it had been becoming. The heart inside the moon was made of the stolen griefs — every one of them — and unwinding the griefs was unwinding it. It was afraid. It was becoming, and she was unbecoming it, click by click.

"Mara." Cael's hand tightened on hers. "It's made of stolen things. You know it is."

"I know," she whispered. And she turned the key backward again.

The voice cried out — not in words, this time, but in the last line of the lullaby, the line that had been lost to her for fifteen years and had come home out of the tooth, perfect and unbearable — and then it thinned, and faded, and was gone. The seam closed to a hairline. The threads went slack.

The tooth was a seed. Black. No bigger than a peppercorn. Still seated in the rim of the moon, pulsing faintly, like the last coal in a dead fire.

Above the river camp, the moon turned — true. No limp. No catch. One-two, one-two, steady as a kept promise.

Silence fell over the trampled grass, broken only by weeping — real weeping, a hundred people weeping with full hearts — and the low hum of the empty cradle, and the torches on the river road, very close now.

The Queen came through the hollow people like a ship through water.

She wore the gray dress, and no crown, and her guards held their torches high and said nothing. She looked up at the true-turning moon for a long time. Then she looked at Mara, standing on the cradle's frame with the key in her fist and tears on her face she hadn't felt fall.

"Three days are ended," the Queen said. "The bargain is kept."

And high above them, the black seed detached from the moon's rim.

It fell slowly, trailing a single silver thread, turning end over end through the moonlight like a thought the sky was having. The thread pointed at Mara — unwavering, certain, the way a needle points at the only thing it has ever wanted.

And from the closed seam, faint as breath on cold air, her mother's voice said: "Catch it, little keeper."

Mara reached up with both hands — and caught it.
