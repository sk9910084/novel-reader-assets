🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 3 · The Paper Courier

The type was warm.

Elias stood with his fingertips on the lead and felt the heat of it, the faint living heat of metal handled minutes ago. Warm type meant someone had set it minutes ago, while he stood upstairs searching the digital files. Warm type meant the room had only just finished being occupied.

He read the chase the way he had been trained to read: proof it. Check every line. MISSING: one elderly printer, name of Elias Vane, last seen at the archive, Saturday last. His wife is dead and asks after him.

"Lena's name," he said to the empty bindery. "You had no right to Lena's name."

The proof beside the chase said, in pencil, in his own young hand: "Saturday."

Today. He was the notice, and the notice was instructions — but the type only named him. It did not say what he was supposed to do.

He went up the stairs and turned the door handle. Locked. He pressed his ear to the wood: nothing.

He came back down, and apprentice memory showed him the room as it had been: the guillotine where it had always been, and behind it, half hidden by a dust sheet, the coal chute.

Elias had been seventeen the last time he climbed through that chute. He was sixty-three now, and the hatch looked narrower than his memory of it. Memory, he thought, dragging the sheet aside, is a poor compositor.

The hatch opened on shrieking hinges onto a black throat smelling of coal dust and the sea. He took the work lamp and went in headfirst, the way you go into any dark place in this trade: committed. Then he was in the alley with coal dust in his cuffs, the fog coming off the water in long white banners.

He let himself back in through the side door — his key still worked, which told him something — and went down to the bindery. If the notice was instructions, he would read it properly: look for what was hidden in the furniture.

Under the proof sheet, tucked between proof and imposing stone where only a printer would look: a slip of paper, folded once, in the same young handwriting:

"The proof is the pledge. Pier end, the blue door, tonight when the horn sounds twice. Come alone. Tell no one — not even the mother."

The mother. Margaret Hale.

He folded the slip into his breast pocket, over his heart. Then he locked the chase in the cabinet where the night girl kept the spare keys and went home through the fog to think.

At home he washed the coal dust off his hands, made tea he did not drink, and sat at the kitchen table — and he understood something that had been waiting since October, patient as type in a case.

The forty-one notices were not epitaphs. An epitaph is for the dead, and nothing in forty-one notices had ever said dead. LOST. FOUND. MISSING — the words of a ledger, not a gravestone. A ledger records withdrawals.

Tom Beck's skiff, the telescope, the Whitaker boy, Bess the collie, the green satchel burned unopened. Forty-one withdrawals from Greyport's record, over forty years. And last month, Thomas Hale.

The boy had not been taken; he had been withdrawn. The camera left at the desk, the call on Tuesday night — a summons, not a lure. Or both. In this trade, Elias was learning, everything was both things at once.

The fog-horn sounded at dusk, once, for the incoming ferry. Elias put on his coat. Tell no one — not even the mother. He hated the instruction and he obeyed it, because obedience was the price of the next page.

The pier ran out into the white like a sentence that had forgotten its ending. The shed with the blue door stood at the very tip, past the last lamp. Elias had printed handbills for the fishing co-op out of that shed thirty years ago; the blue paint was new.

The horn sounded twice.

The door opened before he knocked. The figure was backlit by a single lamp; then it stepped forward into the fog-light, and Elias's heart did the thing it had done in the bindery, at the sight of a page coming right.

"Thomas," he said.

The boy was thinner. A cut was healing on his cheekbone. He wore a fisherman's sweater Elias did not recognize, and he held a camera — a good camera, an expensive one.

"Mr. Vane," Thomas said. "You came alone."

"I was told to." Elias looked past him: a bunk, a paraffin stove, newspapers bound with twine. A life, packed small. "Your mother is sick with it, boy. She thinks you're dead."

"I know. The first month, no contact. They said you'd understand."

"Who is they? The notices, the archive, my name on that chase — what is it all?"

Thomas glanced back into the shed, as if asking permission of someone Elias could not see, then nodded once and began.

The Office, he called it. Not a government — he was careful about that, the way you are careful about a word that could get you killed. A private concern, old as the Gazette, older. It moved people: witnesses, runaways, men with secrets their governments had stopped paying to carry.

And the notices were the ledger. Every withdrawal had to be recorded somewhere no one would ever think to alter, because no one thinks the past is still being written: the bound volumes. Paper doesn't lie about being paper, Thomas said, like a lesson he had been taught. So they print the truth in it — one small notice, a name, a date, a place last seen. Fifty years from now, the record will say this person was here, and then they were gone, and nobody made them gone. They went.

"The handwriting," Elias said. "My dockets. Someone forged my young hand."

"So the archive and the dockets agree, and no one ever finds the seam." Thomas looked at the floor. "He studied your old books for a year. He said your hand was the steadiest he'd ever set beside."

"He. The printer. The one who set forty-one notices. The one who set mine."

"The one who's dying," Thomas said quietly. "In a room above the chandlery. He asked for you by name, Mr. Vane. He said you'd know the weight of a chase."

The fog pressed at the shed's single window. Out on the water, the ferry's engine turned over.

"Why me," Elias said. It was not a question.

"Because the ledger needs a printer," Thomas said. "Because you found the seam, and finding the seam means you were always going to hold it next. Because — " the boy in him showed through the stillness, " — I asked them to make you an offer, Mr. Vane, not a notice."

"What does the offer look like," Elias said, "set in plain type?"

"You print the notices going forward — one every few years, when there's a withdrawal. You keep the dockets, the true ones, in your own hand. And in return — " Thomas lifted the camera, " — people like me get to go on living. Somewhere else. Someone else. But living."

"And if I refuse?"

Thomas did not answer. He did not have to. Elias had set enough type to read the white space: if he refused, the notice about the elderly printer would run — not as an offer but as an instruction. The archive would say he was missing. The fog would keep his secret the way it kept everything.

"Tuesday," Thomas said. "The next issue binds Tuesday. He wants your answer by then." He fumbled in the sweater's pocket and brought out a folded square of photographic paper. "The proof is the pledge, and the pledge is due Tuesday."

Elias took the photograph: Thomas on the pier at dawn, gulls wheeling behind him, the camera held up like a shield. On the back: "Mum — the gulls are good here. T."

"Give it to her?"

"Not yet. After you decide. If you say no, burn it. She can't have hope and then lose it twice."

Elias put the photograph in his breast pocket, beside the slip. Two papers. Two pledges. He looked at the boy and saw the page as it would be set: two columns, two futures, locked up tight, waiting for the ink.

"Tuesday," he said.

The horn sounded once more, far off, for no ferry Elias knew. Thomas stepped back, and the blue door closed between them, gentle as a chase being shut. Elias walked back down the pier alone through the fog, carrying the offer, the photograph, the forty-one names and the forty-second waiting to be set — the way he had once carried hot metal home from the shop. Carefully, because it could burn, and because it was his.
