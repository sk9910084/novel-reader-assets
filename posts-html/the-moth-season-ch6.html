🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 6 · The Moth Season

Ruth did not move toward the word. Fourteen years of keeping hives had taught her never to answer a display before she had counted it. She stood thirty yards off on the marsh road with Eda's brass lamp unlit in her fist, the four letters on the wet stones before her, and let her blood steady.

COME.

It was not for the town. Eda had said it plainly in the farmhouse: tonight it will knock for you at the marsh gate, where it nests. The letters were only the invitation.

She lit the lamp. The moths on the stones lifted at once, a single grey loosening, and poured themselves back down the road toward the farmhouse, the invitation delivered. She set the lamp on a stone at the marsh gate and sat on the damp bank to wait, because a woman who has spent her life tending small fierce things learns early that the only way through a sting is to hold still for it.

Dusk rose off the reed beds the way it did on the marsh, and the mist took the road first at the ankles, then the knees. The reeds clattered, dry as fingers.

Eda's instruction sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: answer with the emptiness of a woman who has nothing left it can take. She had plenty left — Nora on Salt Row, Ada sleeping off a mismeant yes, six hives of dead bees no one else in Dunmere would mourn. So the emptiness was not in the having, she understood, with the sudden clarity of the far edge of fear. It was in the holding. You don't empty the hands. You loosen the fist.

She thought of the hives in the morning the way they used to be, before the display on the bottom boards — the kettle steaming on the stand, the workers fanning at the entrance, her own hand resting on a hive roof, feeling the colony breathe through the wood. Fourteen years of that, gone in a night, and she understood that the emptiness Eda had asked of her was not the absence of love. It was the refusal to bargain with it. If the swarm wanted her, it would have to take her on the merits of her standing there — not her sister, not Ada, not the memory of bees. Just Ruth, on a bank, with a lamp.

The mist reached her elbows. Then, out in the reed beds where the marsh went black against the last of the light, the knocking began. Three soft impacts. A pause. Three more.

It knocked on the air itself, like a knuckle on a door you can't see, from no direction she could name — from the dark, for her. She sat with her hands in her lap, loose-fisted, empty-handed, and let it find nothing in her to land on.

Three. Pause. Three.

Ruth knocked once on the stone beside her.

The sound was small and ordinary. For a moment nothing happened. Then the whole marsh exhaled — a grey tide rising out of the reeds in a single sheet, millions of wings, the sound of them like wind turning pages. Her lamp guttered. She cupped the flame the way she had cupped brood frames in sudden rain, and the swarm rose over the marsh gate and hung there, turning, every moth facing her.

She stood. "Well," she said aloud, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Here I am."

The swarm folded in on itself and settled — on the stones, the bank, the lamp, her shoulders, her hair. It rested on her the way her bees had rested on her veil in better years, a weight like weather. They did not crawl or bite. They were warm, each one a small furnace of held breath, and the weight of them on her hair and shoulders was almost comforting, almost like the press of the hive on a winter morning when she used to lay her forehead against the wood to listen. Then the veil shifted, and through the thin grey of it she saw a figure on the road.

Eda Harrow, in the shawl from the farmhouse bed, walking with the slow deliberation of the very old. She had walked the mile from Harrow Farm on her own two dying legs, and the swarm parted around her as if it had been expecting her all evening.

"Eda," Ruth said. "You were supposed to wait."

"I waited sixty years, Ruthie." Eda stopped at the gate and the moths settled on her shoulders like snow. "I think that's enough waiting."

The weight on her shoulders grew heavier. Inside the grey came a voice like water over stones — not Thomas, not her mother — the sound the marsh made when it wanted something. Three. Pause. Three.

It was asking Eda. Not Ruth. And Ruth understood it all at once, the way you understand a sting the moment it lands: the swarm had never wanted a witness. It had wanted the caller back. Eda had never been offering herself to save the town — she had been paying the oldest debt in Dunmere, and she had asked Ruth to come so she wouldn't have to walk into the marsh alone.

"No," she said, and the word came out of her before she had decided to say it, sharp and human and far too small for the grey enormity in front of her. "Eda, don't."

Eda turned her head. Through the veil of moths her eyes found Ruth's, and they were the eyes of the woman at the funeral sixty years ago, upright as a fence post, who had not cried.

"Child," she said gently, "I already did."

She knocked three times on the air. Three soft impacts. A pause. Three more.

The swarm went utterly still — every wing, every breath of the grey tide stopped, the way bees go still around a queen — and in that stillness Ruth heard the lamp's wick hissing and her own heart going like a fist on a stone. Then, gently as a page being turned, the swarm lifted off her and folded itself around Eda Harrow.

She did not scream. That was the thing Ruth would remember later, lying about least. Eda stood in the center of the grey column with her shawl settling around her like dusk, and looked at Ruth over the rising tide of wings, and smiled.

"He's calling me, Ruthie," she whispered. "I have to answer."

The column turned, rose, and poured itself out over the reed beds toward the black water. The sound it made going was like a held breath finally let go — all those wings, all that grey, folding into the mist in one long exhalation, and the mist closed behind it like a drawn curtain. Ruth stood at the marsh gate with the lamp in her fist and watched, because someone had to watch, because Eda had asked for a witness and she was the only one who had ever been any good at staying still. The last thin ribbon of grey folded into the dark. Then the road was empty, the sky was empty, and the stones were bare and wet and ordinary.

Eda was gone.

Sixty years of waiting, and she had walked into the dark on her own dying legs, and the town would never know. Dunmere would wake tomorrow to clean windows and call it weather, and only Ruth would know that an old woman had paid the oldest debt on the marsh road with her own knocking, three and three. She sat down on the damp bank, because her legs had stopped taking instruction, and she cried — once, dryly, the way she had cried for the bees.

She stood, picked up the lamp, and walked back down the marsh road toward Dunmere. The mist parted around her the way the moths had, a courtesy of the dark, and her boots found the familiar ruts of the road home. Fifty yards on she heard it behind her — not three and three. One soft impact, alone, on the air. Then another. Then another.

One for no, Eda had said, in the old code. The swarm was answering her. Or it was answering itself. Or it was telling her, in the only language it had, that the bargain had been struck — and that a refused summons is only a delayed yes.

She walked faster and did not stop until the lights of Salt Row showed through the mist, and Nora's door opened before she knocked, and her sister's frightened human face said everything the night had not.

But that is for the morning. For now: the marsh road behind her was silent, the stones at the gate were bare, and somewhere out on the black water, faint as wings on glass, something was knocking — once — and waiting to see who would answer.
