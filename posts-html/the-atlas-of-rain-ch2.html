🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 2 · The Atlas of Rain

For three days the Wayward Finch sailed beneath the suspended river, and Iris learned that the sky could be a road if you were brave enough, or desperate enough, to travel it. The rain flowed overhead like a glass highway, silver and endless, carrying a strange cargo along its current. Leaves spun past, green and gold, plucked from forests no chart had ever named. Once a whole school of silver fish poured by above them, swimming through rain as though it were sea, their flanks flashing like thrown coins. The crew took to watching the river the way sailors watch the horizon, with a hunger they did not discuss. On the second evening a paper boat drifted down out of the current and landed softly on the deck, folded from a royal decree bearing the seal of the Meridian Kingdom. Iris smoothed it open with careful fingers and read orders commanding some port she had never heard of to surrender its grain stores before the rains failed. She folded it again and kept it, because a mapmaker keeps everything, and because something about the seal made Soren's jaw tighten when he saw it.

"Meridian," he said, and spat over the rail. "Kings who buy their weather. You'll understand soon enough why your father ran from them."

On the fourth day the river ended, and what it ended at made the crew forget every fear they had ever named. The suspended rain poured itself into a city hidden inside a thundercloud. Pluvia hung in the grey heart of the storm like a secret the sky was keeping. Its towers hung downward, inverted spires anchored to enormous silk balloons that strained and creaked against their cables. Lightning did not strike the city; it traveled through it, running along copper streets and gutters in bright, harmless-seeming streams that lit the windows from below. Bridges of woven cable swayed between the towers, and on them moved people, small as ants, going about their business in a place that should not have existed.

They had barely taken it in when the arrest happened. Copper-hulled skiffs rose from the lower towers, and soldiers in storm-grey uniforms boarded the Finch with the practiced ease of people who had done this many times. Their captain, a woman with lightning-burn scars tracing her cheek like silver veins, looked at the atlas in Iris's oilcloth and her expression changed.

"That book was destroyed with the Rainkeepers," she said. "You will come with us."

They were taken to a chamber lit by bottled lightning, glass jars of captured storm-light humming softly on iron shelves, and there Iris met Archivist Mael. He was a thin man with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had spent too long reading in bad light, and he looked at Iris with an expression she could not parse until he said, "You have his brow. And his stubbornness, I suspect. I was your father's partner, before he decided that conscience was more important than the Guild."

Mael told her the story the way archivists tell everything, as though it were already history. The Rainkeepers had indeed steered storms, but not for glory. The Meridian Kingdom had paid them, year after year, to move rain away from wealthy ports and toward poor islands that could not pay for clear skies. Her father had discovered the ledgers. He had stolen the atlas, the Guild's masterwork, to stop the trade. The blank final page was not empty at all, Mael said. It was a control chart, the last instrument of the Rainkeepers' art. Whoever completed it could redirect the approaching Tempest Crown, the largest storm in a century, which was even now gathering beyond the horizon.

"Draw Greywake outside the storm's path," Mael said, and for the first time his archivist's calm cracked. "That is all I ask. One line, and your home is spared. The storm must go somewhere, child. That is the arithmetic of weather. Better another coast than yours."

Iris thought of the black coastline painting itself over Greywake. She thought of the paper boat's decree, the grain stores, the port that could not pay. She thought of her father's note: trust the water. It has never learned to lie.

"No," she said. "I won't save my home by erasing someone else's. That's not a map. That's a murder with better handwriting."

Mael's face closed like a book. But before he could answer, chaos arrived in the form of Captain Soren, who had spent the interrogation doing what he did best: studying ropes. He cut the balloon anchors of the nearest tower with a deck axe, and the whole inverted spire lurched upward, dragging its cables, swinging slowly through the cloud like a drunk giant. Alarms rang. Soldiers ran. In the confusion Iris slipped away from her guards and into Pluvia's archive, a round room of chart cabinets that smelled of ozone and old paper, and there she found her father's last chart, weighted open on a reading desk as though he had just stepped away seven years ago.

He had marked a route not around the Tempest Crown but straight into it. The margins were crowded with his handwriting, and Iris read with her heart hammering against her ribs. He described a natural breakwater hidden at the storm's center: a chain of airborne islands, torn from the earth decades earlier, whose forests could divide the winds if their great root systems were reconnected. The Meridian had ordered those islands separated long ago, Mael's own records confirmed it, so that storms could be steered like weapons and no natural defense could interfere. Her father had spent seven years trying to undo it alone.

Iris stood among the cabinets and understood, all at once, what the atlas truly was. It did not control the weather. It remembered the shape the weather wanted to take, the way a river remembers its course even after men have dammed it. The Rainkeepers had spent their genius forcing storms into unnatural paths. Her father had spent his trying to let them go home.

She took the chart and ran, but not before one more cabinet caught her eye, its drawer labeled in her father's hand with a single word: GREYWAKE. Inside lay a chart of her own island, drawn with a tenderness that made her throat close. He had marked every cove she had played in as a child, every fishing rock, the attic window of their house with a tiny lantern symbol beside it. In the margin he had written: "For Iris, who draws the world so others can find their way through it. I am trying to make a world worth finding your way through." She folded that chart too, and tucked it inside her coat next to her heart, and then she ran for the docks with lightning gossiping overhead. The Finch was already casting off, Soren shouting orders, the crew hauling lines with the particular fury of people who have been arrested and did not care for it. Behind them Mael gave chase in a copper glider, a wasp-thin craft with a lightning engine that crackled and spat blue fire. The suspended river above them shuddered, broke, and came down all at once, rain crashing into the sea in a white roar, and the Finch dropped through violent waves that rose like fists.

Iris crouched in the stern with the atlas open beneath her coat, shielding it with her body. The ink on its pages was rearranging itself, rivers dissolving and reforming, until they settled into a narrow spiral. At the spiral's center a tiny green mark appeared, no bigger than a freckle. Beneath it, in her father's handwriting, two words bloomed like something alive: "I am here."

She pressed her palm flat against the page and laughed through the salt spray, because after seven years of silence, her father had finally told her exactly where to go.
