🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 3 · The Moth Season

Ada Cline made no sound at all, which was worse than screaming. She sat in Ruth Adler's dark front room with her hand clamped around Ruth's wrist and stared at the window where the moths had finished spelling her sister's name, and the grip of her fingers said everything her voice would not.

NORA.

The grey tide on the glass held its shape for a long moment, every moth hovering a finger's breadth off the pane, wings flat and still. Then, all at once, in a single soundless unbinding, they lifted and folded themselves into the dark, and the window was only a window again.

"The lamp," Ruth said. "Ada. Let go of my wrist. I need the lamp."

Ada let go. Ruth went to the kitchen and came back with her father's lamp burning, the shotgun from the shed, and — on an impulse she would later call instinct and never quite believe — the bee smoker from the lean-to, its bellows cracked and its tin cold. She filled it with screwed-up newspaper the way she had ten thousand times for the hives, and struck a match.

"What is that for?" Ada whispered.

"Smoke calms bees," Ruth said. "Worth finding out if it calms anything else."

They went out into the night together. The cliff path was grey with drifting moths, a thin veil that parted around the lamplight and closed behind them. Ruth walked with the shotgun across her forearm and the smoker smoking in her fist, and Ada walked close enough that their shoulders touched, carrying nothing but her blanket folded over one arm like a shield.

Nora's house sat at the end of Salt Row with its door standing open.

Ruth stopped on the step. The door had been locked that afternoon — Nora had turned the key herself, Ruth had heard it. Now it stood open a hand's width onto a dark hall, and the dark was moving.

"She wouldn't," Ada breathed. "Ruth, she wouldn't go out. Not at night. Not now."

Ruth pushed the door wide and lifted the lamp. The hall was full of moths. They hung in the air in a slow grey column, turning, turning, the way dust turns in a shaft of sun — except that there was no sun, and they were all turning the same way, and at the center of the column stood Nora, facing the back of the house, one hand pressed flat against nothing.

"Nora," Ruth said.

Her sister did not turn. On her shoulders and in her hair the moths had settled like a veil, and she was humming — the tune their mother used to hum over the washing-up, the bell with a crack in it, the one Nora hadn't hummed in thirty years.

Ruth had kept bees for fourteen years. She knew what a queen looked like when the hive went still around her. She knew what it meant when every worker in the colony faced the same direction.

She squeezed the smoker's bellows. A thick white plume rolled out across the hall, and the column of moths shivered. The turning faltered. The veil on Nora's shoulders lifted, confused, drifting sideways on the smoke, and Ruth squeezed again, walking forward into her own kitchen of smoke, until she could reach her sister's arm.

"Nora. Come back."

Nora's eyes were open. They were the wrong kind of open — the gaze fixed on something past the back wall, past the lane, past the town, the way Ruth's own gaze had fixed on the cliff edge the night the swarm spelled her name and the knocking came, three and three, and she had not answered.

Because that was the shape of it. Ruth understood it all at once, the way you understand a sting the moment it lands.

The swarm didn't kill. It called. It spelled the name of the one it wanted, and then it waited — on the window, on the wall, in the dark between the panes — and sooner or later the named one heard it. The Harrow boy in '66, gone by Christmas. Old Widow Pell in '96. The swarm had spelled RUTH first, and come knocking, three for yes, and Ruth had sat in her dark front room with the shotgun across her knees and said nothing. She had refused the summons. So it had moved on. To Nora.

And Nora, who had never refused anything in her life, had opened her door.

"Nora," Ruth said again, louder, and shook her sister's arm, and pumped the smoker until the hall was white and the moths were a grey storm tearing itself apart on the smoke. "Nora, it's me. It's Ruth. The washing-up's done, love. Come away from the window."

There was no window. There was only the dark hall and the back door standing open to the lane. But Nora turned — slowly, the veil tearing off her like wet paper — and her eyes found Ruth's face, and focused, and filled with tears.

"Ruthie," she said, in a small bewildered voice. "Why is it dark?"

Ruth held her up while Ada wrapped the blanket around her shoulders, and the three of them stood in the smoking hall while the moths regrouped outside, settling onto the garden wall in their hundreds, facing the house, waiting. Patient. The swarm was patient. It had waited ten years between callings. It could wait out one night.

They barred the doors and sat in Nora's kitchen with every lamp in the house burning, because the dark was where the spelling happened, and Ruth told them what the vestry registers had told her — summoned, called, taken early — and what her own arithmetic had told her on the cliff path.

"It came three years early," Ruth said. "Which means it was called early. Something summoned it before its time. And it spelled my name first, and I didn't answer, and now it's calling Nora."

Ada's face was grey in the lamplight. "It spelled Thomas in my bedroom," she said. "My Thomas. Nine years in the ground. What does it want with Thomas?"

"I don't know," Ruth said, and hated saying it. "But I know it doesn't make mistakes. That's what Mum said. The swarm doesn't make mistakes."

Nora, wrapped in Ada's blanket, had stopped crying. She was looking at the kitchen window, where the moths had begun to gather again on the outside of the glass, the grey tide rising.

"They're spelling," she whispered.

Ruth and Ada turned. The moths settled, wing to wing, stilled — and the shape formed in the lamplight, letter by letter, with a precision that stopped Ruth's breath.

It was not Nora's name. It was not Ruth's.

ADA.

Ada Cline looked at her own name written in moths on her sister-friend's window, and she did not scream either. She stood up slowly, letting the blanket fall from Nora's shoulders to the floor, and walked to the glass, and pressed her palm against it from the inside, the way Nora had pressed hers against nothing.

"Thomas," she said softly. "That's why it spelled Thomas first. It was showing me. It was calling him, and he couldn't come, so it's calling me instead. Don't you see? It's been calling me all along."

" Ada," Ruth said sharply. "Step back from the window."

But Ada was already turning toward the door, and her face in the lamplight was the wrong kind of calm — the fixed, faraway gaze, the humming starting low in her throat, their mother's tune, the bell with a crack in it.

Ruth moved faster than a fifty-two-year-old beekeeper should have been able to move. She was across the kitchen before Ada's hand reached the latch, and she barred the door with her own body, the smoker still smoking in her fist.

"You don't go," Ruth said. "Whatever it is, whatever it wants, you don't go to it. That's the rule. That's the only rule."

Ada looked at her, and for a moment her eyes were her own — frightened, wet, human. Then the humming rose again, and she smiled, gentle as dusk.

"He's calling me, Ruthie," she said. "I have to answer."

And from outside the barred door, in the dark of Salt Row, came the slow deliberate rhythm Ruth had heard on the cliff path two nights running.

Three soft impacts. A pause. Three more.

Knock-knock-knock.

Three for yes.

And Ada, in a voice that was not quite her own, answered:

"Yes."
