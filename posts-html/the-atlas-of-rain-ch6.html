🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 6 · The Atlas of Rain

The Wayward Finch sailed south before dawn, and Iris Bell stood at the stern watching Greywake sink beneath its ceiling of cloud for the second time in her life. The first time, she had been chasing her father. This time she was chasing a man she had never met, across a sea he was already writing on.

Wren had not been invited, exactly. The sixteen-year-old apprentice had simply appeared on the quay with her wax-sealed satchel and her ink-stained fingers and announced that Archivist Mael had ordered her to go where the atlas went, because someone had to record what happened next, and the school's students were, in her words, 'better at marginalia than at swimming.' Soren had looked at Tomas. Tomas had looked at Iris. Iris had looked at the girl's exhausted, determined face and said, 'She stays.'

By the third day out, Wren had earned her place twice over. She knew the Guild marginalia the way Iris knew coastlines, and on the third evening she spread her copied pages across the great cabin table and explained, in the patient tone of someone who has read the same warning a hundred times, exactly what the Wind Rose could do.

'It does not point north,' Wren said. 'It points to the weather that has been moved. Rain taken from where it belonged. And the hand that holds it can tug a storm the way a lodestone tugs a needle. That is how the Rainkeepers steered for kings. They did not push the clouds. They pulled.'

Tomas went very still. 'And Vey carries it south to the Quiet Eye.'

'To unmake what your father made,' Wren said quietly. 'The fleet will not cut the roots with axes, the way we feared. They will pull the next great storm down onto the islands and let it do the cutting. The roots were grown to divide wind. No one ever tested what happens when the wind is aimed at them on purpose.'

Iris looked down at the living atlas, open on the table between them. The angular hand had been quiet for two days, but the red fleet still sat on the southern coastline it had drawn, waiting like a held breath. She picked up her father's pen.

'What are you doing?' Tomas asked.

'Answering,' Iris said.

She had been thinking about the faint writing in her own hand, the ink that had bloomed beside the foreign fleet before she had touched a pen. She understood it now. The atlas did not only carry writing across the sea. It carried intention. She had been reaching for her pen in that moment, shaping a reply in her mind, and the paired ink had written her thought before her hand could. Which meant the pairing ran both ways. She could write back.

On a blank page she drew, in her own careful hand, a storm course bending away from the Quiet Eye, curving out toward open sea where it could spend itself on nothing but water. The ink held. It was faint, trembling at the edges, but it was hers, and it stayed.

For an hour the page was quiet. Then the angular hand returned, and with a single red stroke it crossed her line out, the way a teacher corrects a student who has misunderstood the lesson.

Iris drew the line again, further out. Vey crossed it out again, harder, the red digging into the paper. A third time she drew it, and this time the crossing stroke came slower, as if the hand across the sea had paused to consider her.

'Stop,' Tomas said. He had been watching from the doorway, and his face had gone the color it had been on the black sand, the day Mael's glider fell out of the cloud wall. 'Iris, stop. That is how it starts.'

'How what starts?'

'The arithmetic.' He crossed the cabin and took the pen from her fingers, gently, the way you take a knife from someone sleepwalking. 'Mael said the same words once. A stronger hand on the instrument. That was his whole argument, and he believed it with his entire heart. The atlas is not a weapon. The moment you use it as one, you are building the next Guild, and you will not even notice the walls going up around you.'

'Then what do we do?' Iris demanded. 'Sail south and watch? He is drawing storms, Father. Somebody has to draw back.'

'Somebody has to tell the truth,' Tomas said. 'That is different from drawing back. You taught me that, or I taught you, I no longer remember which. The Rainkeepers asked the atlas what to command. We ask it what is true. If you start commanding, the book will obey you, and you will deserve everything that follows.'

Wren cleared her throat. Both of them turned. The girl had gone pale, staring at the atlas.

'There is something you should both know,' she said. 'About the cost.'

She told them about the Tide Ledger, the second instrument, burned with the Guild's stronghold twenty years ago. It had recorded the debts of every storm ever steered: the price the weather paid, the drought that followed a stolen rain, the flood that answered a diverted wind. Every line drawn in paired ink was a debt, written down and balanced.

'The Ledger is ash,' Wren said. 'So now there is no one left to record the price. But the weather keeps its own accounts, Iris. A storm that is pulled where it does not want to go will take its payment somewhere. It always does. Draw carefully.'

That night Iris could not sleep. She sat in the stern with the atlas on her knees, watching the blue rivers move restlessly under a sky full of unfamiliar stars. She thought about the port the angular hand had drawn on the southern coastline, and she turned the pages until she found it again: a small harbor marked with the Meridian seal, and beside it, in Vey's hand, a single word she could now read. Sarella.

She knew that name. Soren had spoken it once, spitting over the rail the way he did when he spoke of kings. Sarella had paid for clear skies during the grain riots, years ago. A port that could pay. The paper boat decree from the sky-river came back to her: orders commanding some port to surrender its grain stores before the rains failed. The arithmetic was not beginning again. It had never stopped. It had only been waiting for a new hand to do the sums.

She closed the atlas and went below, and found her father mending a sail by lamplight, his scarred hands moving with the patience of seven years on floating islands.

'You are afraid I will become him,' she said. 'Vey. Or Mael, the way he was.'

Tomas set down his needle. 'I am afraid the book will make it easy. That is worse. It made it easy for all of them.'

'Then help me do it the hard way.' She sat beside him and opened the atlas to the page with the red fleet. 'Not commanding. Telling the truth. The way you did, when you drew the route into the storm instead of around it.'

Together they charted what the weather wanted: the natural courses, the watersheds and winds, the way home for every storm Vey was trying to pull off its path. Iris drew in her steady hand, and Tomas annotated in his, and the paired ink carried their truth across the sea the way it had carried Vey's lies. It was slower than commanding. It had to be argued for, line by line, the way you argue with a friend who is determined to make a mistake.

Near midnight the angular hand returned. It hovered, the ink trembling, over their true chart. For a long moment nothing happened. Then, slowly, deliberately, it wrote beneath the fleet, in words as clear as speech:

'Then come and stop me, little mapmaker.'

Iris felt the blood leave her face. He knew. He had been reading her pages the way she had been reading his, and he was not hiding anymore.

Beneath the words, a new chart drew itself, quick and sure: a narrow strait the Finch had to pass to reach the southern sea, cliffs on both sides like the jaws of something patient. The Salt Narrows. And waiting between the cliffs, drawn in red, five black ships bearing the seal of the Meridian crown.

She ran for the deck, the atlas clutched to her chest, and found Soren at the helm under the cold stars. She showed him the page. He stared at it for a long time, and the weathered lines of his face seemed to deepen as he read.

'He is not sailing to the islands,' Soren said at last, very quietly. 'He is sailing to us.'
