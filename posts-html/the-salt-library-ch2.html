Maya did not sleep. She sat at the kitchen table with the 1996 ledger open in front of her, the brass clock stopped at 4:17, and a mug of tea gone cold, and she read.

The 1996 book was different from the others. The earlier ledgers were cold records: dates, ships, DARK. But 1996 — COLLECTED — read like a confession. Her mother's hand, usually so precise, grew ragged toward the end of that year. Maya found the entry for November 2nd, 1996, and read it three times before the meaning settled.

November 2nd, 1996. The freighter Ostra Light, forty-one souls, rounding the point in fog. Board order 12-B — the light kept dark. But beneath the entry, in the margin, her mother had written: I lit it. I could not watch. I lit the lamp at 23:04 and she cleared the reef with twenty yards to spare. God forgive me. Let the sea take what it is owed.

And beneath that, in the same wet-looking ink that had written Maya's name the night before, a single word: COLLECTED.

Tucked into the back of the 1996 ledger, folded small, was a letter on Lighthouse Board stationery, dated October 30th, 1996. Keeper Rane, it read, you are reminded that order 12-B remains in force for all scheduled passages November 1st through 7th. The lamp will be extinguished 22:30 to 23:30 nightly. Compensation for the inconvenience will follow at the usual rate. It was signed by a man named Harrow. Beneath the signature, in her mother's hand: the usual rate is thirty pieces of silver and I have spent them all on candles for the drowned.

Maya closed the ledger. Her mother had broken the rule once. She had lit the lamp for a ship she was ordered to let die, and the sea had collected something in return. What? Her mother's life? Her mother had died five weeks ago, at sixty-one, of a heart that simply stopped in the night. A natural death. But Maya remembered the funeral, the closed coffin, and Tomas's face — the way the old harbormaster had looked at the coffin the way a man looks at a bill he cannot pay.

At dawn she walked the causeway into town. The tide was low, the wet sand glittering, gulls screaming over the harbour. She went to the Sailor's Rest, the pub that opened at six for the trawler crews, and found Tomas in his corner with a pint he did not appear to be drinking.

"I found the ledgers," she said, without preamble.

Tomas closed his eyes. "Then God help you, girl."

"Tell me about the collector."

He was quiet for a long time. Then he pushed his pint aside and said, "Your grandfather kept the Gullpoint light before your mother. And his father before him. Every keeper of that light has kept the dark nights, going back a hundred years. The Board calls it order 12-B. The town calls it the sea's tax." He looked at her directly. "Some ships the sea wants. The Board signs the order so the insurance pays out clean and the lighthouse keeps its charter. The keeper keeps the lamp dark. And the sea — the sea remembers who held the darkness for it."

"And if a keeper refuses? If she lights the lamp?"

"Then the sea collects the debt another way." Tomas's voice dropped. "Your mother lit the lamp for the Ostra Light in '96. Forty-one souls lived. Three months later, your father drowned in harbour water so calm you could see your face in it." Maya's breath caught — her father had died before she was born; her mother had always said it was an accident, a rope, a tide. "The sea took him instead," Tomas said. "A life for forty-one. The sea is not cruel, girl. It is exact."

Maya's hands were cold around her mug. "The collector came last night. He said the Meridian Star rounds the point in three nights. Three hundred souls. He said I could renegotiate."

Tomas went white. "You didn't agree to anything. Tell me you didn't agree."

"I didn't. But the lamp — it turned last night, Tomas. By itself. The Board cut the power. There's no cable, no generator, and the lens was turning."

The old man stood up so fast his chair fell. "Then she's still in the tower."

"What?"

"Your mother. The light doesn't turn itself, girl." He gripped the table. "Listen to me. If the Meridian Star is the ship, the debt is bigger than anything your family has ever owed. Three hundred souls — the sea won't take a substitute for that. If you light that lamp, it will take you. Not your father, not some stranger. You. The keeper pays in kind."

She tried her phone on the walk back. No signal — there never was signal on the causeway at low tide, something about the cliffs. The ferry would not come until morning, and the Meridian Star would round the point in three nights, and she was alone with a debt she had not agreed to and a lamp that turned by itself. For the first time since the funeral letter arrived, Maya felt something crack open in her chest that might have been grief, or might have been the opposite of grief: the sudden, furious understanding that her mother had not loved the lamp more than her daughter. Her mother had loved the lamp instead of asking her daughter to carry it.

Maya walked back along the causeway with the wind in her face and Tomas's words in her ears, and she did not know what she believed. But she knew one thing: she was going up that tower.

The lamp room smelled of oil and cold. The great lens sat in its housing, still now, dark. Maya ran her hands over the mechanism. The power cable was cut clean at the junction box — the Board's work, that morning, which she had watched. But beneath the junction box, half-hidden behind a panel, she found what the work boat had missed: a hand crank, old iron, its handle worn smooth by decades of palms. Her mother had kept a way to turn the lamp by hand. For the dark nights, Maya understood — or for the nights she chose to defy them.

She found the oil reservoir too, still half full. The lamp could burn. All it needed was a keeper willing to climb the stairs and crank.

She stood in the lamp room as the afternoon light went grey through the glass, and she thought of the Ostra Light, forty-one souls who had lived because her mother could not watch them die. She thought of her father, drowned in calm water. She thought of three hundred people on the Meridian Star who did not know that a woman they'd never met was deciding whether they lived.

And she thought of the last line in the ledger, writing itself in wet ink: CHOOSE.

That was when the knocking started — not at the cottage door this time, but above her, on the glass of the lamp room. Three times. Slow. Deliberate.

Maya turned. Outside the glass, impossibly, stood the collector — not on any ledge, not on any ladder, but on the air itself, his oilskin streaming seawater that fell upward into the sky. He pressed one pale hand to the glass.

"Midnight," he said, and his voice came through the glass as if it were not there. "The price is named at midnight, keeper's daughter. Come to the reef at midnight — alone — and hear what three hundred souls will cost you."

The glass fogged where his hand had been. When it cleared, he was gone.

Maya looked down at her own hand. Without thinking, she had picked up a nub of chalk from the lamp-room sill — her mother's chalk, for marking the lens — and on the iron housing of the great lamp she had written, in her mother's own hand without knowing it, a single word:

LIGHT.
