🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 3 · The Clockwork Moon

They escaped through the machine's cable tower, and Mara would remember the climb all her life: the iron rungs slick with condensation, the cables humming above them like plucked strings, the whole tower swaying as the great watch strained against stopped time. Cael went first, then Orin, frail but stubborn, refusing all help, then Mara with the key clenched in her fist. Behind them, the Queen's voice rang out, giving orders Mara chose not to hear. Above them, the golden ladder still hung from the moon's face, swaying in a wind that did not touch the earth, and when they reached the tower's crown the ladder's lowest rung was there waiting, as if it had known they were coming.

The ladder carried them up, and Lume fell away beneath them into a map of lanterns and silver stillness. Mara did not look down after the first minute; she fixed her eyes on the rungs and counted, as Orin had taught her, and Cael's boots scraped above her, and Orin's breathing came thin and determined below. Halfway up, the cold bit through her coat, and the stars—so near now they seemed within reach—burned with a hard white fire she had never seen from the ground. Orin faltered once, his hand slipping, and Cael dropped three rungs in a heartbeat to steady him, and Mara felt a sudden fierce warmth toward the young captain that had nothing to do with trust and everything to do with the sight of his hand clamped around her master's wrist. They rested a minute on the swaying ladder, three small figures between earth and moon, and Orin, catching his breath, pointed upward with a trembling finger. "Look," he whispered. And Mara looked, and saw the moon's face opening before them like a door. The air grew cold, then colder, then strangely warm again, tasting of copper and starlight. And then the rungs ended, and they stepped—there was no other word for it, though every sense protested—onto the face of the moon.

Inside, it was vaster than any cathedral. Gears the size of houses turned with geological slowness around a dark central axle that rose into shadow above and sank into shadow below. Chains of linked stars hung in festoons. And everywhere, descending from the mechanism toward the distant earth, ran thousands of silver threads, each no thicker than a hair, each trembling faintly. Mara touched one as she passed, and felt, unmistakably, a heartbeat: quick, frightened, alive. Every thread was tied to a living heart in Lume. The moon was not merely the city's clock. It was the city's pulse, made visible.

At the rim, jammed between two great silver teeth, sat the black gear. Up close it was worse than through the telescope: matte, lightless, faintly vibrating, and wrong in a way that made Mara's teeth ache. Orin laid a trembling hand near it—not touching—and his face went gray. "It isn't broken metal," he whispered. "It's grief. Grief made solid." He turned to Mara, his eyes bright with horror and understanding. "The Queen's machine feeds it. Every bottled second, every stolen moment of sorrow, flows up the cables and into this. And it grows, Mara. It grows whenever anyone below wishes hard enough to undo a loss."

As if summoned by his words, a sound came from the ladder: the Queen, climbing alone. No crown. No guards. No mourning black, only a plain gray dress, and the miniature portrait clutched in one hand. She stepped onto the moon's face and looked around at the turning gears with the exhausted wonder of someone who has stopped pretending. For a moment no one moved. Then she walked to the black gear and placed her bare hand flat against it, and the moon groaned—a sound like a mountain clearing its throat—and lurched backward by a single tooth.

Below, on the earth, an entire street vanished.

Mara felt it through the soles of her feet, a lurch and a wrongness. Through the open clock-face she could see Lume's eastern quarter shiver like a reflection in disturbed water, and when it settled, Marrow Lane—with its dyers' shops and the little tea house where Mara had once celebrated her apprenticeship—was gone, replaced by buildings from the previous winter, whole and wrong, their windows dark. A year, unmade in a single tooth. The Queen stared down at what she had done, her hand still pressed to the gear, and she was weeping without sound.

"Your Majesty, stop!" Mara seized the Queen's arm and pulled, but memories were pouring from the black gear now, spilling across the moon's face like water: the prince laughing in the palace orchard, sunlight in his hair; the Old Bridge on parade day, bright with banners; the cracking sound, the screams, the water closing over; the Queen running through the crowd, arriving a single breath too late, her shoes lost in the mud. Mara lived them all in heartbeats, grief layered on grief, and nearly let go.

It was Cael who broke the spell. He stepped forward into the spilling memories, his young face wet, and said clearly, "I ignored the warning. The bridge-master sent word the supports were rotten, and I was late to the parade ground, and I never passed it on. The prince died because I failed him." The Queen turned. Her hand rose and struck him across the face, hard enough to stagger him—and Cael did not raise his hands, did not step back, did not defend himself at all. He simply stood, and bled from a split lip, and waited. The Queen looked at him for a long moment, her hand still raised, and slowly lowered it. Punishment, Mara saw her understand, could not turn guilt into repair. It could only make two broken people where there had been one.

Orin, who had been examining the axle's base with his lens, called out hoarsely. "Mara! Here—a socket. Narrow, beside the axle. The key will fit." They gathered around: a dark keyhole no wider than a finger, set into the moon's own substance. "But someone must hold the black gear clear while the key turns," Orin said. "It will fight. It knows what the key can do."

Cael stepped forward at once. "I'll hold it."

"No." Mara caught his arm. She had seen what the gear did to grief; she would not feed it a young man's life. "We do this together, or not at all." She unbuckled her belt. Cael, understanding, unbuckled his. Orin produced his tools, and Mara eyed the golden ladder where it hung beside the clock-face. "The ladder too," she said. "Weave it in. It's strong enough to hold the moon's own weight."

They worked feverishly, hands numb in the moon-cold: belts and tools and strands of the golden ladder braided into a makeshift brace, wedged between the black gear's teeth to hold it clear of the silver wheels. It would not hold long—Mara could see the strands trembling—but it might hold long enough. The Queen watched them for a long silence. Then, slowly, she unfastened the fine chain that held her son's portrait, and laid it across their brace, adding its strength to theirs. She met Mara's eyes, and in that look was everything: grief, and shame, and a terrible, dawning hope.

Mara set the iron key in the socket. It fit as if the moon had been waiting since the world began. She turned it.

The black gear cracked. Not all at once, but in a spreading fracture of light, and from the cracks poured a storm of dark fragments—each one, as it fell, becoming a quiet memory before dissolving: a hand held, a song half-remembered, a laugh in an orchard, sunlight on water. Thousands of griefs, released, gentled, let go. The moon lurched beneath their feet. Every silver thread rang at once, a chord so pure it hurt, like a harp the size of the sky. And time—Mara felt it more than saw it—time rushed back toward Lume like a tide returning, carrying the stolen moments home.

Their brace was failing. The golden strands frayed, the belts slipped, and with a sound like a sigh the ladder snapped loose from the clock-face and fell away into the dark, its rungs scattering like thrown coins. The moon's great shutters were beginning to close. Dawn was approaching, and they were stranded on the face of the moon with no way down.
