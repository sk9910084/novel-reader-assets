Maya sat down.

The chair was her mother's chair, at her mother's table, in the warm lamplight that had no business burning in a cottage with no power. Across from her, the young woman from the photograph on the mantel — dark hair, sea-grey eyes, the keeper's oilskin folded over the back of her chair — went on writing in the 1996 ledger, COLLECTED, her pen moving in the same hand Maya had read all night in the cellar.

"You came back too soon," her mother said, without looking up. "The price isn't finished being named, Maya. Sit down. There is a third column in the ledger, and no one has ever shown it to you."

Maya's hands were shaking. "You're dead. I buried you. Closed coffin."

"I know, love." Her mother turned a page. The paper sounded like a small wave breaking. "I'm not here the way you're here. The tower keeps a little of everyone who ever kept it — that's the part Tomas never told you, because he doesn't know. But listen. We don't have long. The crank is turning, and when it stops, I go back to it."

"To the lamp."

"To the lamp." Her mother finally looked up, and her eyes were exactly as Maya remembered from the good summers, before the silence got long between them. "You read the ledgers. Date, ship, DARK. But you never asked what the narrow column on the right was for. Look."

She turned the 1996 book toward Maya. The entries ran in the familiar hand: November 2nd, 1996. The freighter Ostra Light, forty-one souls. Board order 12-B. Light extinguished 22:30–23:30. And then, in the narrow right-hand column, in the ragged hand of that terrible autumn: I lit it. I could not watch.

"No," Maya said. "That's not a column. That's a note."

"Read the column beneath it." Her mother ran her finger down the page. Entry after entry, the narrow column held short lines in the same wet-looking ink that had written Maya's name on the last page. ONE HUSBAND. PAID. Beneath another: SPARED — THE HARBOURMASTER'S BOY, WINTER OF '74. Beneath another: GIVEN — CALM SEAS, ONE WINTER.

Maya's throat closed. "What is this?"

"The sea's arithmetic." Her mother's voice was steady. "The third column is where the keeper writes what the sea took — or what the sea gave — in exchange. The sea is exact, Maya. Tomas told you that much true. Every darkness kept is a coin borrowed. But the sea pays its debts too, in its own currency. Your grandfather kept the dark faithfully for thirty years, and the winter your uncle's boat went down in fog off the point, the sea set it gently on the sandbar instead of the reef. He wrote it down. GIVEN."

"And the Ostra Light?" Maya whispered.

Her mother's hand rested on the page. "I lit the lamp for forty-one souls I was ordered to let die. The sea named its price." She looked at Maya, and her eyes were wet. "I wrote it myself. That's the rule no one tells you, love. The only choice is what you offer. If you name the price yourself, the sea will not name it for you. I wrote my husband's name so the sea would not take my daughter."

The brass clock on the wall said 4:17. It had said 4:17 for thirty years. Maya stared at it, and understanding came up through her like cold water through sand.

"4:17," she said. "The minute you wrote it."

"A memorial to a minute," her mother said softly. "The minute I stopped being a wife and became only a keeper."

Maya thought of the letter tucked in the back of the 1996 book — order 12-B, signed by a man named Harrow, the usual rate. "The Board pays for the dark nights," she said slowly. "The insurance pays out clean. That's what Tomas said. My family has been — we've been part of a fraud. A hundred years of it."

"Yes." Her mother did not look away. "The Board sells ships to the sea, and the sea sells them back to the Board as wrecks, and the keepers in the middle keep the books. That is what order 12-B is, love. Everyone's hands are dirty. But the sea's side of the bargain is ending — not because the Board wishes it, but because the account is almost paid. One willing keeper closes it. And when it closes, the Board's orders become what they always were underneath: just paper. They will come for the light, Maya. Men like Harrow don't surrender a hundred years of clean payouts because the sea changed its mind. If you write your name, you won't only be keeping the lamp. You'll be standing between the Board and the reef, every night, for the rest of your life."

Maya closed her eyes. She could feel the pen in her fingers, cold as the hand that had slid it to her.

"You kept me away," Maya said. "All those years. The missed birthdays, the unanswered calls. You kept me away so the book would never learn my name."

"I kept you away so the book would never learn your name," her mother agreed. "But it learned it anyway. The night I died, it wrote you in itself. I'm sorry, Maya. I kept the tower between us for thirty-four years, and the tower took you in the end anyway."

Above them, through the cottage ceiling, through the tower's stone, came the slow, stately sound of the great lens turning on its hand crank. Someone was keeping the light.

"That's you," Maya said. "Up there. You've been keeping it since the night you died."

"Somebody has to turn it." Her mother's smile was tired beyond tired. "I'm tired, love. Twenty-eight years of dark nights, and five weeks of keeping the light for a daughter who hadn't decided yet. I'm so tired."

Maya looked down at the ledger. Her mother had turned to a fresh page — ruled in the familiar three columns, the ink still wet. At the top, in the wet-looking hand: THE MERIDIAN STAR. THREE HUNDRED SOULS. BOARD ORDER 12-B. And in the second column: DARK.

The third column was blank.

"No keeper has ever written in it beforehand," her mother said. "They've all waited, and let the sea collect. But the column can be written before the night, Maya. A price named in advance, offered willingly. The sea prefers its payments living — Tomas warned you, and he was right, but he doesn't know what living means. It doesn't mean your death. The dead pay nothing."

"What does it mean?"

Her mother reached across the table and took Maya's hand. Her fingers were cold as seawater. "It means a keeper. Willing. You write your own name in the third column, and you light the lamp for the Meridian Star with your own hand, on the hand crank — and the whole account closes. Not just the Star. All of it. A hundred years of dark nights. The Board's order dies with the bargain, because the sea will have no more use for it, and no more use for keepers who keep the dark."

Maya's breath caught. "And the cost?"

"You keep the light." Her mother's voice was very quiet now. "Every night. For life. You never leave Gullpoint — not for a day, not for an hour — until the sea releases you, which is when you die. And there will be no next name. The line ends with you. That's the real price, love. Not your death. Your life. And your children's freedom — the sea will never learn their names, because there will be no book left to write them in."

The crank turned above them. Slower now. Maya could hear it faltering — the long pauses between turns growing longer, like a heart running down.

"She's stopping," her mother whispered. "Listen. When the crank stops, I go back to it, and the choice goes back to being yours alone, the way it was always going to be."

Her mother slid the pen across the table. It came to rest against Maya's fingers.

The ledger lay open. The third column stood blank and wet, waiting. Above, the great lens turned — paused — turned again, each revolution slower than the last, the light sweeping out to sea and back, out and back, patient as a heartbeat, patient as a mother.

"Write," her mother said, "or don't. But decide before the crank stops."

Maya picked up the pen.

Above her, the turning faltered — caught — and held its breath.
