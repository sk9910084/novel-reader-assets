🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 3 · The Signal Below

The Nereid still had her ballast jets on the manual circuit — short, brutal bursts of compressed air that could nudge the dead submersible in any direction. Each burst cost them breathing time. Lena did the math on the cabin gauge with a cold feeling in her stomach: forty minutes of air at rest, less if they exerted themselves, and the manual gate was a kilometer away along the sphere's curving inner wall.

"We crawl," she told Arun. "Short bursts. We ride the drift between them."

Arun nodded, already pulling on his thermal layer beneath the dive suit. "And Voss?"

As if summoned, the captain's voice filled the cabin, stripped of all pretense now. "Ortiz, your telemetry shows you adrift. Return to the drill site with a reef sample, or I cut your surface support. No winch, no decompression chamber, no rescue. You'll surface on your own or not at all."

Lena looked at the comms panel for a long moment. Then she did something that would end her career at Helix Energy forever: she switched the transmitter to the open emergency channel — the frequency every vessel, coastal station, and maritime institute on the planet monitored — and began broadcasting everything. The reef images. The hollow sphere. The severed branch. Her own voice, steady and clear, describing exactly what Helix had drilled into and what would happen if the pressure gate failed.

"Secrecy protected Chorus for thirty years," she said into the microphone, while Arun stared at her with dawning admiration. "My mother kept it secret to keep it safe. But now the only thing that can protect it from Helix is witnesses. So I'm giving it witnesses."

On the surface, nine miles above, she knew Voss would be watching his own screens fill with incoming acknowledgments — fishing boats, research stations, a Japanese oceanographic institute, a Chilean naval relay, a university lab in Lisbon whose night-shift technician would later say she cried when the images resolved — and she smiled for the first time in hours.

The pressure gate was worse than Chorus had shown them.

It rose from the sphere's floor like a wound in the world: a ring of black stone thirty meters across, surrounding a crack in the seafloor that glowed orange from beneath, the light of the superheated current pressing up from the crust. Helix's extraction pipe — a steel artery as thick as a redwood trunk — had pierced the ring's edge and driven deep into the fracture. With every pulse of the drilling machinery on the surface, the crack widened by a fraction, and each fraction sent a shudder through the water that Lena felt in her bones.

Arun suited up in the reinforced exosuit, checking the cutting charges with methodical, trembling hands. The plan was simple and terrible: swim to the pipe, attach the charges at the stress points, retreat, detonate, and let the broken pipe fall away so the gate could close. Simple. Terrible.

He went out through the lock, a tether line connecting him to the Nereid, his suit lamp a small star in the vast dark. Lena watched from the viewport, her hands on the manual jet controls, ready to nudge the submersible after him. He reached the pipe. He attached the first charge, then the second, his movements slow and precise in the weakened gravity.

Then the current took him.

It came from the crack itself — a surge of superheated water escaping through the widening fracture — and it caught Arun like a hand catching paper. He slammed against the black stone ring hard enough that Lena heard the impact through the hull, a dull metallic clang, and his suit lamp spun crazily as he tumbled. His tether snapped taut, then began to fray against the ring's edge.

Lena didn't think. She was in her own suit in ninety seconds, through the lock in two minutes, her own tether clipped to the Nereid's hull. The water outside was warm as blood. She fired her suit thrusters in short bursts, fighting the current, and reached Arun where he hung against the ring, dazed, his faceplate cracked in a spiderweb pattern but holding. She clipped her tether to his harness, doubling their lifeline, and together they clawed their way along the ring toward the pipe.

Chorus could not help them directly — the projection had told the truth; human metal was dead and invisible to its cells. But the blue organisms came anyway, thousands of them, gathering around the two divers in a living constellation. They clustered thickest at three points along the pipe's base, pulsing brightly, and Lena understood: weak points. Stress fractures. A living blueprint drawn in light, showing them exactly where to cut.

They placed the charges where the organisms glowed. They retreated to the limit of their tethers. Lena thumbed the detonator.

The pipe shuddered, screamed — metal tearing under nine miles of pressure — and broke free at the base. For one glorious second, the crack in the gate began to close, the black stone ring contracting like a muscle.

Then it stopped. A shard of drilling steel, a meter long, had lodged crosswise in the fracture, and the stone could not seal around it. The orange glow pulsed angrily. The current surged again.

Their air gauges were in the red. Arun's breathing was ragged in her helmet speaker. Lena looked at the dead Nereid drifting nearby, its robotic arm still extended from the sample attempt, and made the decision before she could be afraid of it. She fired her thrusters, reached the submersible, and manually cranked the arm's joints — no power needed, just leverage — until the claw gripped the steel shard. Then she threw her whole body against the arm's housing, using the submersible's mass as a ram, and drove the claw forward.

The shard tore free. The ring sealed with a sound like the Earth clearing its throat — a deep, resonant boom that rolled through the water and through their bones. The orange light vanished. The current died. Where the crack had been, there was only smooth black stone, and the blue organisms settled over it in a slow, reverent spiral.

But the Nereid was caught. The sealing stone had closed around the submersible's extended arm, and then around its hull, gripping it like a hand closing on a coin. Lena stared at her ship — her home for the descent, her only way back — being swallowed by the gate it had helped to save.

"Leave it," Arun gasped. "Lena, leave it."

She left it.

What happened next, Lena would never be able to explain to the committees. The water around them warmed suddenly, impossibly, and a column of it began to rise — a great slow pillar of warm current, lit from within by thousands of blue organisms, lifting both divers gently as if they weighed nothing. Chorus was carrying them. The membrane parted above them like the eyelid had parted below, and they rose through nine miles of dark water in a cocoon of light and warmth, their failing air suddenly irrelevant, their cracked faceplates somehow holding.

They surfaced under a sky full of stars, miles from where the Meridian held station. A fishing boat was waiting — an old trawler out of Guam, its captain having received Lena's emergency broadcast and steamed at full speed toward the coordinates. Rough hands hauled them over the rail. Someone wrapped them in blankets that smelled of diesel and salt. Someone else pressed a warm mug into Lena's hands and then a radio handset, and through the static she heard it: institutions around the world, already copying the reef data, already mirroring the telemetry, already publishing.

Behind them, the sea bulged upward once — a great, slow dome of water rising like a held breath finally released — and then settled into perfect calm.

Voss could no longer erase what thousands had seen.
