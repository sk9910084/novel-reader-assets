🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 5 · The Clockwork Moon

Two winters had passed since the long night, and Mara Vale had learned that some repairs take longer than others. The city kept time truly now; the clocks on the workshop walls ticked their patient chorus, and the river murmured beyond the shutters as it always had. But Orin's hands never stopped trembling, and the white in his hair stayed, and each month when Mara climbed to the observatory to listen to the moon through the great telescope, she pressed her ear to the brass and waited a little longer than before, the way one waits for an old friend to finish a sentence.

She went on the full moon, as always, alone. The astronomer met her at the door with his customary bow and his customary complaint about the cold, and she climbed to the dome and fitted her ear to the listening tube and let the moon speak to her. The gears turned with geological slowness. The silver threads chimed faintly, thousands of living heartbeats strung between earth and sky. And beneath them all, the thin dark seam where the grief had cracked the moon lay quiet as a healed scar.

Then she heard it: a knock. Hollow. Off-rhythm. A single tooth of the great mechanism striking wrong, the way a loose tooth clicks in a gear-train just before it fails. Mara lifted her head and put her eye to the lens instead, and the moon leapt into terrible clarity, silver and engraved, turning around its dark central axle. The seam was still there, thin as a hair. But beside it, at the rim, something had changed. Where the black gear had sat, wedged and broken, the silver teeth were whole again. And in one of them, no bigger than a thumbnail, was a spot of matte black. Not a crack. A growth. As if a splinter of the shattered grief had taken root and begun to remember how to be a tooth.

Her hands were shaking when she found the astronomer at his charts. She told him what she had heard and seen, and he went very still, and together they went through the lunar logs line by line, month by month, and found nothing. The instruments recorded a perfect moon. "The machines cannot hear what the moon tells its Keeper," he said at last, and there was something in his voice that might have been apology and might have been fear.

She asked him, then, what the logs said of the seam itself, and he turned the pages back two full years and showed her: a thin ink line on the chart of the moon's face, annotated in his own hand, fading fainter every month. "It heals," he said. "That is what the glass says." But his eyes did not meet hers when he said it.

Mara walked home under a sky going pale toward morning, and did not sleep. She lay in the narrow bed above the workshop and listened to the clocks ticking below, every one of them true, and thought about the hollow knock among the moon's gears, and about how grief, once, had been a gear of pure black wedged between silver teeth, and about how the city had believed, for two whole years, that broken things stayed broken once they were mended. Toward dawn she rose and took the iron key down from its nail and held it in her palm until the metal warmed, and put it back. The danger had never been the key. The danger was whatever the key had opened, and whether it had learned to open on its own.

The spring fair came to Marrow Lane that week, and the whole street turned into a river of color: silk banners snapping in the wind, spice-sellers shouting their prices, a juggler with seven brass balls, children with painted moons on their cheeks. Mara went because the workshop needed lamp oil and because staying home meant staring at Orin's trembling hands and remembering the knock she had heard through the listening tube. She was haggling over oil when the crowd shifted, and she saw the stall. She had come to Marrow Lane a hundred times since the street returned from unmaking, and she knew its shopfronts the way she knew her own tools: the dyers with their vats of indigo, the tea house with its low door, the baker whose ovens had never once gone cold in the two years since dawn returned. But the black stall was new, and wrong, and Mara felt the wrongness the way a clockmaker feels a bent tooth in a train she has not yet opened. The fair swirled around it, bright and oblivious—children darting between the banners with painted moons on their cheeks, a spice-seller crying his prices in a voice like a cracked bell, the smell of roasted chestnuts and lamp-oil and rain that had not yet fallen—and at the end of it all, the mourner's stall, glowing.

It stood at the end of the lane where the tea house had returned from unmaking, draped in black canvas like a mourner. On its shelves, ranked in velvet-lined boxes, glowed hundreds of small glass bulbs. Each one pulsed with faint light. Each one, Mara knew with a cold that ran to her fingertips, was a bottled second.

A woman sat behind the stall with coal-dust brows and a smile like a well-oiled latch. "Bottled hours," she called. "Joy for the weary. Borrow a laughing morning. Borrow the exact smell of rain." A thin crowd had gathered, watching, half-hoping, half-afraid, and Mara watched with them, her fists clenched inside her sleeves. A laundress with red raw hands stepped forward and laid down three copper coins. The vendor selected a bulb, warm and golden, and held it to the woman's ear. The laundress gasped, and laughed, and wept all at once. "My girl," she whispered. "My little girl laughing. She is grown and married now, and I had forgot how she sounded at six years old." She clutched the bulb to her chest like it was bread.

Mara's throat closed. Because she knew that laugh had been stolen, once, from that very laundress, by the Queen's harvesters, in the years when sorrow paid for time. And she knew something else, worse: the machine had been dismantled. The seconds had been released. The shelves in the service tunnel were empty. So where had this one come from?

She waited until the crowd thinned, and then she stepped to the stall and laid her hands flat on the black canvas. "Where do you get them?"

The woman's smile did not falter. "From folk who sell them freely, Keeper. We pay good coin for strong memories. Grief fetches the best price, mind." She leaned closer, conspiratorial. "Joy fades by morning, everyone knows that. But grief, Keeper—grief keeps. Grief is the one coin that never spends itself."

Mara bought the cheapest bulb on the shelf, a small dim one, and carried it home wrapped in her handkerchief like something that might bite. In the workshop she set it on the bench and pressed her ear to the glass, and what she heard froze the blood in her veins. A child's laugh, bright and recent, echoing off stone walls Mara recognized as the new bridge's arches. Not a memory returned from the long night. A moment harvested within the last week. She listened again, longer, and heard beneath the laughter the unmistakable rush of the river through the bridge's new piers—piers that had not existed a year ago. The harvest was not some dredged remnant of the Queen's old store. It was happening now, in living Lume, to living people, while the city slept.

Someone in Lume was bottling seconds again, and the grief was being fed to something that grew, up there, on the face of the moon, one matte black tooth at a time.

That evening the vendor's last words followed her up Scholars' Hill and would not leave her alone. She had asked where the grief went, all that purchased sorrow, and the woman had smiled her oiled smile and pointed one finger at the rising moon. "Up," she had said. "To the new buyer."

Mara stood on the observatory steps and looked up at the moon hanging huge and silver over the darkening roofs. The seam crossed its face like a held breath. And at the rim, where no instrument would confirm it, the black spot had grown since midnight. It was the size of a thumbnail now, and Mara could have sworn, in the failing light, that it was vibrating. Faintly. Eagerly. Hungry.

She thought of the Queen in her gray dress, striking the first blow against her own machine. She thought of all the grief Lume had released and forgiven and let go. And she understood, with a lurch like a missed stair, that forgiving grief was not the same as destroying it. Somewhere, someone had gathered up the splinters.
