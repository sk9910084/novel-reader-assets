🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 4 · The Moth Season

"Yes."

The word left Ada's mouth and the knocking stopped — not finished, but cut off in the middle of its pattern, three soft impacts and then nothing, as if a hand had been lifted away mid-thought. The silence that poured in afterward was so complete that Ruth could hear the lamp's wick hissing.

Ada sagged. All the wrong calm drained out of her at once and she folded into Ruth's arms like a coat off its hook. Ruth went down with her, Nora catching her other side, and between them they got her into the chair. Her eyes were open. They were her own again — frightened, wet, human — and then they closed, and she slept sitting upright with her head tipped back, the humming dying in her throat until it was gone.

Ruth stood over her with the smoker still smoking in her fist and understood, with the cold clarity of the far end of fear, that the swarm had gotten what it came for.

"Ada?" Nora whispered. "Ada, love, can you hear me?"

Ada's breathing stayed deep and even. On the kitchen window the moths had begun to lift — not scattering, but unbinding all at once, the way they had on Ruth's window two nights before, a single soundless loosening — and the glass was only glass again, lamplight and dark beyond it.

Ruth went to the window. The street outside was empty. The garden wall where the moths had massed in their hundreds stood bare in the lamplight, the stones wet and black. Salt Row looked like any lane in Dunmere at midnight: a place nothing had ever happened to.

"They're gone," Nora said.

But Ruth was a keeper, and keepers listen for what the quiet is covering. She stood at the glass with her forehead nearly touching it, and after a moment she heard it: far off, above the rooftops, a sound like wind turning pages — the whole swarm, moving at once.

"Come here," she said.

They stood together at the window, Nora's hand on sleeping Ada's shoulder, and watched the sky over the church tower fill with a slow grey column, turning, turning, every moth in Dunmere rising in a single thought and pouring north along the north road, toward the marshes.

"They're going," Nora breathed. "Ruth — they're going home."

Ruth watched until the last of the grey had folded itself into the dark. Then she said, "No," because her beekeeper's mind had snagged on the one fact that would not sit still. "In the morning they came pouring over the headland from the direction of the town. The vestry records say the swarm comes from the marshes beyond the north road. These came three years early, and out of the wrong quarter entirely."

Nora stared at her. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying they're not going home. They're going back to where they were called from. And whatever called them isn't in the marsh." Ruth turned from the window. "It's in Dunmere. It always was."

They kept the lamps burning and took turns watching Ada, who slept on through the small hours with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a service, peaceful in a way that frightened Ruth more than the humming had. At half past three — the hour of the knocking, the hour the moths had come to Ada's bedroom window — Ada opened her eyes.

She was herself. Ruth saw it in the first second, the way you know your own bees: the wrongness gone from her face like a shadow lifting off water. Then Ada began to cry — ordinary, ragged, human crying — and Nora held her while Ruth banked the lamp and sat down across from them, because there are conversations you have to have sitting down.

"I said yes," Ada whispered. "Ruthie, I said yes to it."

"I know, love."

"I didn't mean — I meant Thomas. I thought if I answered, it would take me to Thomas." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "But it didn't want me. That's what I remember. Under the wings there was a voice like water over stones, and it was Thomas, and he said — he said, 'Not yet, love. Not you.'"

Nora's hand tightened on Ada's shoulder.

"Why did it spell my name, then?" Ada said. "If it didn't want me, why did it write me on your window?"

Ruth thought of the bedroom in Salt Row, the name traced in the dust of a window that had never been double-paned. "Because when it spelled his name in your bedroom," she said slowly, "you wanted to go to him. The swarm heard the wanting. It doesn't make mistakes — that's what Mum always said. It spelled your name because you were already answering."

Ada nodded, tears still coming. "And then I did answer. And it let me go." A shudder went through her. "But Ruthie — it came three years early. It doesn't do that on its own. Something called it."

"The rule," Nora said. "It's called when someone's dying."

"Thomas told me." Ada's voice dropped. "Out on the marsh road. There's a woman out there, and she's been calling for weeks. She keeps a lamp burning all night, and she calls a name into the dark, over and over, and the moths can't help but go to a light." She looked up. "She's dying, Ruth. He said she's dying, and she knows it, and she won't stop calling."

Ruth felt the cold move up her arms the way it had in the apiary, standing over the neat concentric rings of her dead bees. "What name is she calling, Ada?"

"I couldn't hear it." Ada shook her head. "The wings were too loud. But Ruthie — the swarm spelled your name first. Before Nora's. Before mine."

The kitchen went very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on Salt Row, a long way off, the sea chewed at the cliff.

"If she's calling names," Ruth said, "and mine was first —"

"Then she's calling for you," Nora finished.

Ruth sat in the lamplight and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the vestry. Taken early, the old vicar had written in the margin in 2016. The swarm was taken early this time because the summoning was early. And the summoning was early because out on the marsh road, a dying woman had been calling and calling for weeks, and the moths had finally been unable to wait.

But the names. The swarm spelled the name of the one it wanted, and it had spelled RUTH first, on her own window, in letters a hand's width tall. Ruth ran through every woman she knew on the marsh road and found the list short and the possibilities shorter. The marsh road held the old coastguard cottages, empty since the eighties, and Harrow Farm, empty since 1966 — since the Harrow boy, the first name the swarm ever spelled, gone by Christmas.

Her mother had been dead eleven years. Her father longer. There was no one left on the marsh road who knew Ruth Adler's name.

Except, said a small cold voice in the back of her mind, the woman with the lamp.

Dawn came grey and clean over Dunmere, and the town woke to windows washed clear by the night's fog, as if nothing had happened at all. Ada slept on, truly slept now, in Nora's spare bed. Nora wanted to come, but someone had to stay with Ada in case the moths came back to Salt Row — so Ruth went alone up the cliff path in the thin morning light, the smoker cold in her fist, the shotgun left behind on Nora's kitchen table, because you cannot shoot a summons.

The north road ran straight as a rule out of Dunmere and into the marsh, and the marsh in October was a grey sea of reeds with the mist lying on it in banks. Ruth walked with her shoulders set against the wind; the only sound for a mile was her own boots and the far-off tearing of the tide.

Harrow Farm stood where it had stood for sixty years, its roof sagging, its windows blind — except one.

In an upstairs window, against the grey morning, a lamp was burning.

Ruth stopped in the middle of the empty road. As she watched, the last thin ribbon of the swarm — the stragglers, the late ones, a grey thread no thicker than smoke — came pouring down out of the brightening sky and folded itself, moth by moth, through the dark of the farmhouse door, like water finding a drain.

The lamp burned on. And from somewhere inside the blind and empty house, faint as wings on glass, came the sound of a woman calling a name.
