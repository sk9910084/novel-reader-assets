🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 11 · The Atlas of Rain

The Wind Rose was warm as bread in Iris's hand, and it weighed nothing at all.

She had expected the instrument of the Meridian Office's reckoning to feel like a manacle — brass and gravity and arithmetic. But Vey's compass sat light in her palm, its needle steady now, pointing not east to the ruined fleet or south to the Quiet Eye, but downward, through the deck of the Wayward Finch, through the hull, through the sea beneath.

"Down," Iris said.

Vey nodded, and he looked strangely unburdened for a man who had just surrendered his office's oldest instrument. "The Rose points at debt," he said. "Not north. Not true anything. It points at whatever owes weather the most."

"The First Root," Wren said, bent over the atlas, "draws itself fainter every time I look. Like it's afraid of being found."

Iris had spent the debt — paid it twice over on the king's fleet and the king's vaults. But a birthplace could not be spent. That was the trouble with origins: you could burn the ledger, you could drain the casks, but the root that grew the weather still drank from somewhere.

Soren came up from the cable tier with his jaw set the way it got before weather. "We don't have the luxury of philosophy, mapmaker. Look astern."

Iris turned. On the eastern horizon, beyond the capital's headlands, the sea had darkened in a long gray band — sails, dozens of them, the color of wet ash, each flying the Office's seal. The Grey Fleet. Not the wrecked fleet she had spent the storm on. The other one. The one kept in reserve.

"Forty hulls," Soren said. "Against one brigantine and a woman with a warm compass."

Tomas joined them at the rail, his scarred hands braced on the wood. He had said little since the harbor, since the atlas went quiet in his daughter's hands. Now he looked at the gray sails the way a man looks at an old creditor at the door. "They'll run us down before nightfall if we stay on this heading."

"Then we change the heading," Iris said. "Down."

The Rose pointed down — no coast, no heading, because nobody had ever sailed to the First Root. To reach it, you didn't sail outward. You sailed under.

"The cloud sea isn't water," Vey said quietly. "It's the sky's underbelly. Ships don't sail under it."

"The Finch isn't entirely a ship," Iris said. "Not since the Tempest Crown taught her to fly."

Soren stared at father and daughter for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, barking, unhinged sound — and turned to bellow orders across the deck. The Finch swung her bow away from the open sea and toward the pale band of cloud low on the southwestern horizon, where the sky lay down to meet the water and no line separated them.

Wren caught Iris's sleeve. "The marginalia has a warning. About sailing the cloud sea." She flipped the Guild book open with shaking hands. "'Below the weather lie the waters where weather is unmade. No keel passes that does not leave something of itself behind.'"

"Something," Iris repeated. "Like what?"

Wren looked up. "It doesn't say."

The Finch reached the cloud band an hour before dusk, with the Grey Fleet a gray rumor on the eastern horizon. Up close, the place where sea became sky was a slope — a long pale incline of mist that the bow climbed like a swell, and then the masts were in it, and then the decks, and the world went white and cold and silent.

When the mist thinned, the sea was above them — a vast silver ceiling flecked with green — and the Finch rode on nothing but cloud, the weather itself holding her keel, for once, like a friend. Below them — where the sea should have been — there was only depth, pale and falling away, and in that depth, far below, something dark and enormous branched like the roots of a drowned tree.

"Oh," Wren breathed. "Oh, that's the chart."

The First Root. Iris had drawn it faint and uncertain from a dimming atlas. Here it was, larger than any of her lines had dared: a root wider than the Finch was long, rising out of the pale abyss, its bark the color of old thunderheads, its surface etched with channels that ran with slow silver light. And from its crown, impossibly, rain was falling upward — climbing away from the root into the silver ceiling, where it spread and became the sea above.

"That's where rain comes from," Tomas said, very softly. He had his hand on the rail, and Iris realized he was not looking at the root. He was looking at the channels. "I turned storms aside for seven years, girl. I always wondered where they went when I moved them. Now I know. They came home."

The Rose grew hot in Iris's hand. The needle swung — from the root, to her father, and back again. Down, to the dark where the root plunged. Iris followed the needle's direction with her eyes and felt her stomach drop out from under her.

The root had been cut.

Not cleanly, like the engineers' saws had cut the bridges. This was old. The wound was enormous, wider than the Finch's deck, and it had scabbed over with something that was not bark — something smooth and silver and artificial, like a lid. Cables thicker than a man's arm ran from the lid down into the abyss, and at the far end of the cables, hanging in the pale depth like a spider in a web, was a shape of black iron and canvas: a vessel. An Office vessel. Older than the Grey Fleet. Older, perhaps, than the crown itself.

"They didn't just harvest the bridges," Vey whispered. "The First Root is the source. If the Office could cap it — meter it —" The tired clerk was gone from his voice, and what was left was horror. "They could tax the rain itself. Every coast. Every field. Every throat."

A bell tolled from the iron vessel, faint across the depth. A single note. Then a flag broke from its masthead — gray, with the old seal of the Office, the seal from before the Meridian crown.

"They've seen us," Soren said. He was already shouting orders. "All hands —"

But the Finch had no wind. On the cloud sea there was no wind at all; the air hung still as a held breath. The sails slatted uselessly. And below, on the iron vessel, something was moving along the cables — small figures, ant-small at the distance, swarming up toward the root's crown.

"They're going to open the lid," Wren said, her voice thin. "Iris. If they open the lid and take the source —"

Iris opened the atlas.

The pages were gray. The rivers were quiet. But the book remembered everything, and on the last bright page the chart of the First Root glowed faint — faint but there. Enough for one more chart. Maybe.

"One chart," she said. "One. Not to spend. Not to command." She looked at her father, at Soren, at Wren, at Vey. "To show."

She took up her father's pen — no, her own pen, the one she had drawn Greywake with seven years ago — and on the last bright page she drew not a coast, not a heading, not a debt. She drew the truth: the root, the lid, the cables, the iron vessel, and above it all the rain climbing home to the sky. She drew what the Office wanted and where it had hidden its oldest sin, and she drew it the way she had drawn every honest blank space in her life: exactly where the truth ran out, and then, bravely, past it.

The page blazed. Once. Bright as bread from an oven.

And on the cloud sea, above the First Root, the mist began to move — not toward the Finch, but toward every ship that had ever owed weather anything. Toward the iron vessel below. Toward the gray band of the Grey Fleet, far above and east. The weather was coming to look at its own birthplace, and it was bringing witnesses.

Iris closed the atlas. It was empty now. Truly empty. The covers were cool and ordinary in her hands, a book at last, and nothing more.

The Rose cooled too. Its needle settled, steady as a held breath, pointing down — at the root, at the lid, at everything she had just shown the sky.

From below, the iron vessel's bell tolled again. Closer. On the Finch's deck, Soren's crew stood at their stations with no wind and no orders, watching the mist gather and darken into something vast and patient and enormously curious.

Iris Bell lifted the Wind Rose like a lantern, and sailed her father's ship down toward the root of all weather, to meet whatever the Office had left guarding the rain.
