🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 1 · The Atlas of Rain

Iris Bell made maps of places she had never seen, and she had made peace with that long before anyone thought to question it. Her attic sat above the harbor of Greywake like a lantern above dark water, its wide windows facing the sea and its floorboards worn smooth by seven years of pacing. From that height she drew deserts from sailors' descriptions, coaxing dunes out of the way a deckhand's hands moved when he talked about heat. She drew mountain passes from merchants' complaints, because a merchant complaining about a road would tell you its every treacherous turn without realizing he had handed you the whole route. She drew coastlines from the gulls' flight lines and harbors from the smell of their ropes. What she could not draw, she left as honest blank space, and the blank spaces on her charts were famous among captains for being exactly where the truth ran out.

Greywake itself she knew by heart and therefore almost never drew. It was a small island beneath a ceiling of clouds that rarely lifted, a place where ships came for repairs and left before sunset because the tides after dark turned strange. The harbor smelled of tar and salt and wet wool. Children learned to read the sky before they learned their letters. Iris had learned both, and then she had learned the harder lesson: that her father, Tomas Bell, the finest chartmaker the island had ever produced, had walked out one grey morning seven years earlier to map the legendary Quiet Eye, a place said to command every storm, and had never walked back.

She kept his chair exactly where he had left it, angled toward the window. She kept his brass dividers in their felt case. She did not keep his promises, because she had stopped believing in those the winter the search ships came home empty. What she kept, fiercely and without apology, was his conviction that a map was a kind of promise too, one you made to strangers you would never meet: I have been careful here, it said. You can trust this line.

The parcel arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in sailcloth and tied with tarred twine, carried up the attic stairs by a dock boy who said a fishing smack had found it lashed to a piece of wreckage two miles out. The address was written in her father's handwriting. Iris knew it the way she knew her own pulse. She sat down very suddenly on the floor, because her knees had decided the matter before her mind could catch up, and she turned the parcel over twice before she could make her fingers work the knots.

Inside was an atlas with no printed title. The cover was a deep sea-blue leather, worn at the corners, and when she opened it her breath caught in a way that had nothing to do with grief and everything to do with wonder. The rivers on the pages were moving. Thin blue lines slid across the paper like living veins, rearranging coastlines she recognized and inventing ones she did not. Rain had begun to patter on the attic roof, and with every gust the rivers shifted, as if the weather outside were redrawing the world in real time. She touched one with a fingertip and felt the faintest warmth, like the page was breathing.

Tucked into the binding, folded small, was a fresh note in the same beloved hand: "Maps do not show where the world is. They show what someone wants you to believe. Trust the water, Iris. It has never learned to lie."

She read it four times. On the fourth reading she noticed the final sheet of the atlas, and her wonder curdled into something colder. The last page was entirely blank except for a single compass rose, and the compass rose was spinning, its needle whirling toward the sea beyond her window as though it could not decide which direction fear lived in.

Before she could study it further, the harbor bells began to ring. Not the slow bronze tolling that marked a ship's arrival, but the rapid, ragged alarm that Greywake reserved for tides that broke the rules. Iris ran to the window and felt her stomach drop. The water was withdrawing. It pulled back from the harbor walls in a long, impossible exhale, exposing black rocks slick with weed, and then the wrecks: three sunken ships appearing on the naked seabed like accusations, their masts tilted, their hulls crusted with seven years of silence. Gulls screamed and wheeled. On the horizon rose a wall of cloud stretching from one edge of the world to the other, bruised purple and green, moving with a deliberateness that made it seem alive.

She looked down at the atlas in her hands. New ink was blooming across the final blank page, spreading like frost in reverse. A black coastline was painting itself directly over the shape of Greywake.

Her hands were shaking, but her training held. A mapmaker does not panic at a new coastline; a mapmaker asks what it means and who drew it. This coastline had drawn itself, and it meant that whatever was coming wore the shape of her home like a target. She wrapped the atlas in oilcloth, slung her father's old satchel across her shoulder, and ran down the attic stairs two at a time into streets already filling with frightened people.

There was only one sailor on Greywake who would leave harbor before a storm instead of after it, and his name was Captain Soren. She found him on the quay, watching the withdrawn sea with his arms crossed, his weathered face unreadable. His ship, the Wayward Finch, was small and fast, a sloop with patched sails and a figurehead some long-ago carver had given up on halfway through, so that it resembled a bird only if you squinted with charity. Her crew was a collection of people who trusted weather less than they trusted one another, which is to say not much, but they trusted Soren, and on the sea that was usually enough.

Iris thrust the atlas at him without preamble. Soren unwrapped the oilcloth, looked at the moving rivers for a long moment, and went very still in the way of a man hearing a name from a grave.

"This belonged to the Guild of Rainkeepers," he said quietly. "Mapmakers who were accused of steering storms for kings. They vanished twenty years ago, and good riddance, most people said. Your father was one of them, wasn't he?"

"He was a chartmaker," Iris said, and it came out sharper than she intended. "He made maps. He didn't steer anything."

Soren looked from her to the black coastline spreading over Greywake, and then to the wall of cloud on the horizon. "That black coast will swallow my home too," he said. "That's the only reason I'm saying yes. We leave on the flood tide, which gives us perhaps an hour. Bring warm clothes and everything you know about that book, which I suspect is nothing yet. You'll learn on the water."

They sailed with the returning tide racing beneath them, the Finch skimming over the drowned wrecks as the sea rushed back to swallow them again. Behind them Greywake shrank beneath its ceiling of cloud. Ahead, the compass rose in the atlas stopped its frantic spinning all at once, as if it had finally made up its mind, and pointed northwest with absolute certainty.

Then Iris saw it, and for a moment she forgot to breathe. Above the ocean, stretching from one cloud bank to another, ran a river of rain. It did not fall. It flowed sideways through the sky like a glass road, a narrow silver path pouring from cloud to cloud, carrying leaves and twigs and impossible things along its current. The crew stared in silence. Even Soren, who had seen thirty years of sea, had nothing to say.

Iris opened the atlas to its first page with hands that had steadied the way they always did when there was work to do. New ink was appearing even as she watched, dark and deliberate, forming words in her father's hand across the moving rivers: "To reach the eye, follow the water that never falls."

She looked up at Soren. He looked at the impossible river. Then he put the helm over, and the Wayward Finch turned toward the sky-road of rain, toward the northwest, toward whatever her father had found seven years ago and never come home from.
