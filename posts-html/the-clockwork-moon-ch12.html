🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 12 · The Clockwork Moon

The listening tube was cold against Mara's ear, and the moon was singing with two voices.

She had come up to the observatory alone at midnight, the way she had every night since the seed rose, to learn the small heart's moods the way a keeper learns a mechanism. The first voice was the one she knew: her mother's lullaby, whole and kept, sung by a heart the size of a peppercorn nine miles above the sleeping city.

The second voice sang the second line.

It was thin and new and uncertain, and it came a breath behind the first, the way a child follows a teacher — and Mara lifted her head from the tube with her heart hammering, because the second voice was not in the moon.

It was in the city.

She found Cael waiting at the foot of Scholars' Hill with a lantern, though she hadn't asked him to come. "You heard it too," he said. In the three nights since the heart had begun to sing, half of Lume had heard something at the edge of sleep.

"Not half," Mara said. "One. One voice, answering. The heart is teaching its song to someone, and someone is singing it back."

Her mother's compass hung at her belt. She took it out. The needle pointed at the moon — unwavering, certain. She turned slowly, watching the needle, walking the dark streets while Cael held the lantern high. Down Scholars' Hill, through the sleeping market, along Flour Row — and there the needle trembled, swung, and pointed down the row, at a narrow house with blue shutters, where a single candle burned in an upper window.

The lamplighter's house. Old Bram had lit the street lamps for forty years and never missed a night, even the night that would not end.

The candle was in his daughter's window. Lissa. Nine years old. Mara had seen her a hundred times, chasing pigeons in the market square.

"She's singing in her sleep," Mara whispered.

They could hear it through the shutters, faint as breath: the second line of the lullaby, perfect and unbearable, in a child's high clear voice.

Cael put his hand on her arm. "Mara. What does it mean?"

"It means the heart is lonely," she said, and her throat closed. "It means it's reaching down, the way it reached for me, and finding the nearest open window. A sleeping child can't close her ears, Cael. She can't choose. And the heart doesn't know the difference between a song given and a song overheard."

She knocked. Bram came down in his nightshirt, lamp in hand, and let them up when she told him who she was — the Keeper; everyone knew the Keeper now.

Lissa slept with the blanket kicked off and her face to the window, to the moon. She sang the second line again in her sleep, sweet and certain, and on the sill beside her bed, Mara saw it: a single silver thread, fine as spider silk, rising from the sleeping girl's lips toward the sky.

"No," Mara whispered.

She had seen threads come down to grief. She had never seen one rise from a dream.

She sat on the edge of the child's bed and sang — awake, on purpose, her voice rough with midnight — the first line of her mother's lullaby, the way her mother had sung it to her.

The thread trembled. The second voice faltered — and then the heart took up Mara's awake singing instead, and the thread from Lissa's lips thinned, and thinned, and let go, dissolving like frost in sun.

Lissa sighed in her sleep and turned over, silent. Bram stood in the doorway with his hand over his mouth.

"Will it come back?" he asked.

"Not tonight," Mara said. "I gave it something better. A song sung awake is —" She stopped, because she didn't know how to explain keeping to a lamplighter at midnight. "It's heavier. The heart can feel the difference. It just didn't know."

But walking home under the true-turning moon, Cael said what she was thinking. "There will be other windows, Mara."

"I know."

"And you can't sit on every child's bed in Lume."

"I know." She looked up at the moon, at the hairline seam, at the place where the heart beat. "The heart is growing, Cael. Orin said a starving heart does desperate things. It's not starving anymore. It's learning. And a heart that learns too fast —"

"— cracks its chamber," Cael finished. Orin had said it that morning, his face grave: the seam was widening again. "A heart that grows too fast cracks its chamber, and then where will we be? Back to the tooth, or worse."

They found Orin waiting at the observatory, and he was not alone.

The merchants had come back.

Mara recognized the tall one from the hollow days — the man who had priced Petra's wedding day with a smile like a ledger. He bowed, and behind him two clerks unrolled a cloth covered with gold coin.

"Keeper," the merchant said. "We hear the moon sings now. We hear it has a heart." His smile was still a ledger. "The tooth ate grief, and grief is cheap — everyone has some to sell. But a heart that sings? A kept song, freely given? What would the great houses pay for a lullaby that never fades? We are prepared to be very generous."

"You want to buy the moon's song," Mara said flatly.

"We want to buy the rights to listen," the merchant corrected smoothly. "A small bell, in a nursery. A thread, no thicker than this —" he held up a finger and thumb, nearly touching — "carrying the song down from the sky. In every city. Think of it, Keeper. No child ever unsung again."

It was, Mara realized with horror, almost beautiful. And it was almost exactly what the tooth had been: a machine for carrying human feeling away from humans, priced and packaged.

"No," she said.

"Keeper —"

"No." She stepped between the merchants and the listening tube, the Lunar Key warm at her wrist. "The heart sings because it is kept, not because it is sold. The day I price it is the day it starts eating again. Ask Orin. Ask anyone who was hollow."

The merchant's smile did not move, but something behind it did — a calculation, closing like a ledger. "The heart is growing, Keeper. Hearts that grow need feeding. If you won't sell its song, what will you feed it when it outgrows lullabies?"

He left the gold on the cloth. Mara didn't touch it.

After they were gone, Orin listened at the tube for a long time. "Three voices now," he said quietly.

Mara's blood went cold. "Where?"

"Flour Row. Tanners' Lane." He looked at her. "My clever girl — the heart is dreaming, and the city is dreaming with it. You can't sit on every bed in Lume. So you must do what keepers do when a mechanism outgrows its housing."

"Which is?"

"Build it a bigger housing." He took her hands, his trembling fingers warm. "The long winding, Mara. Not feeding — teaching. Every full moon, the kept songs, sung awake, on purpose — but not just yours. The city's. You must teach Lume to sing to its moon the way a mother sings to a child: awake, willing, together. A chorus, not a whisper. Give the heart so much freely-given song that it never needs to steal a dream again."

Mara looked up at the true-turning moon. Three small voices rose with her mother's lullaby, sweet and dreaming and unaware.

"And if the city won't sing?" Cael asked.

"Then the heart will keep reaching," Orin said. "And one night it will find a window that doesn't close."

Mara climbed to the observatory roof alone. She put her ear to the night air and listened to the three sleeping voices, and then she did what her mother would have done: she sang. Awake. On purpose. The whole lullaby, start to finish, her voice carrying over the sleeping roofs of Lume.

One by one, the sleeping voices faltered — and stilled.

And then, from the moon, the heart sang back — alone, the way it had at first. Content. For now.

But as Mara turned to go, the listening tube breathed a new sound: not the heart, not the lullaby. A voice — small, human, wide-awake — speaking from somewhere high and far.

It said: "The lady in the moon taught me the song. She says she's lonely. She says she wants to come down."

Mara froze. "Who is that?" she whispered. "Who's speaking?"

And from nine miles above the sleeping city, the voice answered, patient as a kept promise:

"Ask your mother."
