🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 2 · The Vermilion Ledger

Nihar Banerjee woke before the alarm and lay a moment counting the ceiling fan's rotations — eleven before the kettle murmured. He wrote the number down. Paper was the only witness that never got transferred.

He set out his notebook, his pencil, his reading glasses, and began the slow copying work from the red ledger: date, name, amount, interest, due date, the margin word recovered, the red thumbprint beside each name like a small wound.

Eleven names. He checked each against the obituary columns of the morning paper and the longer obituary column of his memory, and wrote LIVING or DEAD in his own margin. Three dead: Tapan Ghosh, aide to a Bowbazar councillor, found at the foot of the Sealdah flyover last month — ruled an accident, four lines in the newspaper; Sailen Dutta, who had owned three buses on the Howrah route, dead of a "heart attack" in a Tollygunge flat in August, aged fifty-one; Bimal Mondal, pulled out of the Hooghly near the Howrah bridge in June, his pockets full of stones. Beside each death date he wrote the recovery date. Nine days. Six days. Eleven days. In every case the money had come in just before the man went out.

Then he added the column.

The pencil moved down the figures the way it had moved ten thousand times before, sure and unhurried, and the total came out clean — too clean — in a way that lifted the hairs on his forearms. Twenty-three lakh, sixty thousand rupees. ₹23,60,000.

He sat very still. Then he went to the steel almirah and took down the file he had not opened in three years: his own final memo from the 2019 Saha investigation, eleven months of arithmetic across three seized diaries and two half-burnt registers, the failure that had followed him into retirement like a debt of his own. He turned to the last page. The gap he had never been able to close — disbursements exceeding recorded receipts, money lent that had never come home on paper — stood there in his own handwriting, underlined twice.

₹23,60,000. To the rupee.

The thumbprint loans were the missing money. Saha had been running a second ledger inside the first one — and twenty-two days ago, the man keeping it had walked out of his shop and not come back.

He locked the red ledger in the almirah, slipped the matchbox with the cigarette stub into his shirt pocket, and took the tram to Bowbazar.

The tram swayed down through College Street with the morning crowd, and Nihar counted the stops without meaning to — nine of them — until the conductor called Bowbazar and he stepped down into the lane-smell of the old money market: frying oil, damp jute, brass polish, and underneath it all the faint metallic tang of counted cash.

Saha & Sons stood halfway down a lane so narrow the sky was a ribbon. The brass nameplate was green with age, the shutter down, the padlock rusted the orange-brown of months — undisturbed, its hasp furred with corrosion. No hand had touched it in weeks.

Nihar stood looking at the padlock for a full minute, because the padlock was telling him something and he wanted to hear all of it. The parcel had been wrapped at Saha's counter, by hands that knew brown paper and string. But this shutter had not been lifted in weeks. So the counter work had happened somewhere else — or the shop had another way in.

Across the lane, a tea stall hissed. The boy working it could not have been more than sixteen, with the watchful eyes of someone who misses nothing because his livelihood depends on it.

"Chhotu," Nihar said, reading the name painted on the stall's flank, "Saha babu's shop. Always closed?"

The boy poured tea into a clay cup without being asked. "Twenty-two days, uncle. Since the day he didn't come. Police came, police went." He jerked his chin at the shutter. "Nothing moves there."

"Nothing? No one at all?"

Chhotu glanced up and down the lane, and Nihar recognized the look of a witness deciding what a thing is worth. Nihar put a ten-rupee note on the counter and left it there, the way you leave a door open.

"Three nights ago," Chhotu said quietly. "Very late. A black car at the end of the lane, engine running, no lights. A man came out of Saha babu's side door — not the shutter, the side door, the one for the godown — carrying something flat. Brown paper. Tied with string." The boy's eyes flicked to Nihar's face. "Like a parcel, uncle."

Nihar drank his tea to give his hands something to do. The side door, set in the wall beside the shutter, was painted the same peeling green, its mortise lock showing none of the padlock's rust. Someone had a key. Someone had been inside the missing man's shop three nights ago, wrapping something at his counter.

"Did you see his face?"

"Coat," Chhotu said. "In this heat, uncle — a coat. And he walked like the lane belonged to him."

Gouranga Pal sold brassware two lanes over — trays, water pots, gods with polished faces — and he was polishing a tray when Nihar came in, a man in his fifties with brass dust in the creases of his hands. Nihar introduced himself as retired police, accounts branch. Retired, he said again, gently. Not a raid. A question.

He showed Pal his notebook copy — not the ledger, never the ledger. Pal stared at it a long time.

"I paid," he said at last. "Every rupee. One lakh fifty thousand, in cash, at his counter. I have the receipt." He fetched a tin box, and from it a slip of paper: cash received, Saha's upright signature — and beside it, pressed hard, a thumbprint in red ink. Pal's own thumb.

"He started it six months ago," Pal said. "The red thumb. He said it was the old way. For protection, he said. For luck." A short unhappy laugh. "I thought he meant my luck."

"When did you pay?"

"Four months ago." Pal put the receipt back in the tin box as if it might bite him. "Banerjee babu — Ghosh, Dutta, Mondal. We all paid in the same month." He leaned forward, voice dropping. "Ask the others if you want. But ask quickly. Men are forgetting their own names in this lane."

"Did Saha say where the money went? After you paid?"

Pal looked at the door of his shop, at the lane beyond it, and then back at Nihar. "He said the red ink remembers what the black ink can't afford to." He picked up his polishing cloth with hands that were not quite steady. "I didn't ask what he meant. I am asking now, every night, and there is nobody to answer."

Nihar walked back toward the tram depot with the afternoon heat on his shoulders, and stopped at a glass-fronted cigarette shop near the crossing. He set the matchbox on the counter and opened it.

The shopkeeper picked up the stub between thumb and forefinger. "Black-and-gold filter. Imported. Two hundred rupees a stick, uncle. Only two shops in the city sell them — mine, and one in New Market." He set it down carefully. "The kind a man smokes when he wants you to know he can afford it."

Nihar thanked him and bought nothing. Evidence was not for smoking.

He reached home at dusk and counted the marigolds along the courtyard wall out of pure habit — nineteen, two wilting, the same as the morning. The almirah was locked. The ledger was safe. He made tea and sat down with his notebook to write up the day: the undisturbed padlock, the side door, the black car, the eight living names, the receipt in the tin box, the red thumb for protection.

The landline rang.

It had not rung in weeks — months, perhaps; Nihar counted everything, which told its own story. He let it ring twice while the tea cooled, then picked it up.

"Banerjee babu." The voice was unhurried, educated, entirely calm. "You are adding it up again."

Nihar said nothing. In thirty-one years he had learned that silence is also a question.

"Stop," said the voice, "and page forty-seven stays missing."

The line went dead.

Nihar stood with the dead receiver in his hand, listening to the dial tone the way he had once listened to confessions, and counted his own heartbeats until they steadied — forty-one before the tone stopped sounding like a warning.

Then he sat down, and wrote that down too. Because the voice had known about page forty-seven — and only three people in the world knew about page forty-seven: Saha, missing twenty-two days, the man who had wrapped the parcel, and Nihar himself.

Unless, Nihar thought, setting down his pencil very carefully, the sender and the watcher were not the same man at all.
