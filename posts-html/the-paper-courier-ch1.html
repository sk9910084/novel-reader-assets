🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 1 · The Paper Courier

The fog came in off the water at four o'clock, the way it always did in October, and by half past five the windows of the Gazette archive were blind with it. Elias Vane did not mind. He had spent forty-one years setting type in rooms that smelled of ink and machine oil, and the archive basement — paper dust, damp stone, the faint sweetness of old glue — felt like a second trade picked up late in life.

He was sixty-three, five years retired, and he volunteered three afternoons a week at the Greyport Gazette, feeding bound volumes into the scanner the county had paid for. Digitize the past, the young editor had said. Make it live forever.

Elias privately doubted the past wanted to live forever. The past, in his experience, preferred the dark.

The volume on the cradle was October 1987. He turned to page six, smoothed it under the glass, and stopped with his hand still on the page.

The classifieds were wrong.

He knew this page. He had set it himself on a wet Tuesday thirty-nine years ago — page six, lower half, a quarter-column box for Harlow's Bakery: "Fresh rye daily. Ask for Marta." He remembered the chipped 'y' in 'rye' and swapping the sort from the California case with fingers gone numb from the cold. He remembered Marta Harlow paying in cash and a paper bag of warm rolls, and his wife Lena laughing when he brought them home, saying the bakery would be the ruin of his teeth.

The scanner showed a different page. In its place sat a two-line notice in plain eight-point type:

LOST: one grey skiff, name MARIGOLD, last seen moored at the south pier. Reward. Enquire at this office.

Elias stared until the scanner's light timed out. He lifted the glass, checked page five, then page seven. Both were exactly as he remembered. Only page six was wrong.

He logged out, closed the volume, and drove home through the fog to the green filing cabinet in his kitchen — the job dockets of Vane & Son, Printers, every job from 1978 to the spring he closed the shop. A printer keeps his dockets the way a priest keeps his confessions; Lena had teased him about the cabinet for thirty years, and after she died he had kept it the way some men keep a grave.

October 1987. His own handwriting, blue ink gone brown at the edges: "Gazette 12 Oct, p.6 low: Harlow's Bakery, ¼ col. Paid 18.50."

He sat at the kitchen table a long time, the fog thickening beyond the window. Then he put on his coat and drove back through it to the archive.

The night girl had gone; the desk lamp burned alone. Elias pulled the 1987 volume down again and went through it page by page, slowly, the way you check a forme for a dropped letter. Page six was the only wrong one.

He pulled 1994. Then 2003. Then 2011.

In each volume he found one wrong page. Always in the classifieds. Always a small notice — LOST, FOUND, MISSING — in plain type, naming a boat, a dog, a brass telescope. And always, when he checked his dockets at home the next morning, his own records showed a different ad had run in that space. A bakery. A plumber. A church jumble sale.

Someone was rewriting the archive, one page at a time. Someone with access to the bindery, the paper stock, the patience of years.

It took him three weeks to see the pattern. He made a list in the back of a docket book, in pencil, and the list frightened him more than the tampering had:

Oct 1987 — LOST: grey skiff MARIGOLD, south pier.

Jun 1994 — FOUND: one brass telescope, on the cliff path.

Mar 2003 — MISSING: collie dog, answers to Bess.

Nov 2011 — LOST: green canvas satchel, ferry dock.

He remembered every one of them. That was the terrible part. The Marigold had belonged to Tom Beck, and Tom had taken her out in the storm of October '87 and never come back; Elias had printed the funeral notice himself, and wept over the type. The telescope was old Mrs. Abernathy's, left on the cliff path the week before they found her at the foot of it. Bess the collie had vanished the same spring the Whitaker boy ran away to the city and never wrote. The green satchel — he had never learned whose it was, only that the ferry office kept it unclaimed for a year and then burned it, unopened, for the insurance.

Every notice named something that had vanished within days of the issue date. The ads were not advertisements. They were epitaphs — printed after the fact and slipped into the bound record like flowers into a coffin.

He told no one. Who would he tell? The editor was twenty-nine and thought letterpress was a font on his phone. The police would ask for evidence, and his evidence was a feeling in his fingertips, the memory of type he had set with his own hands forty years ago.

Instead he started coming in on Thursdays too, and then on Saturdays, working backward and forward through the volumes, building his pencil list. There were forty-one notices. Forty-one vanished things, over forty years. He dreamed about them — small grey print swimming up out of black water — and woke with the taste of ink in his mouth, the way he had in the old days after a long run.

The forty-second notice was in last month's volume. September 2026.

He found it on a Saturday morning with the fog pressing at the windows and the radiators ticking. Page nine, lower half, where the church notices should have been:

MISSING: one young man, name of Thomas Hale, last seen at the ferry dock, Tuesday last. His mother asks after him. Enquire at this office.

Elias read it three times, and the third time his hands were shaking. Thomas Hale was twenty-four years old. He sat at the scanner next to Elias on Thursday afternoons, drank his tea with too much sugar, and called him Mr. Vane no matter how often Elias said Elias. The boy wanted to be a photographer. He had shown Elias pictures of gulls over the pier, and Elias — who had not praised anything in years — had said they were good.

Thomas Hale had not come in on Thursday. His phone went straight to voicemail.

Elias closed the volume and sat very still, listening to the radiators. Then he drove home through the thinning fog, went to the green cabinet, and pulled September 2026 with fingers gone clumsy.

There it was. His own handwriting — but he had not written it. He knew he had not written it the way he knew his own name, the way he knew Lena's laugh, the way he had known every lie the type had ever told him.

"Gazette 14 Sept, p.9 low: MISSING notice, Hale. Set from copy rec'd. Paid — Vane & Son account."

Vane & Son had been closed for five years. The account did not exist. No copy had ever come to him.

Someone was still setting the notices. Someone was using his name to do it. And the latest one named a boy who was missing now, today, while the fog pressed at the windows and the phone in Elias's pocket stayed silent as a stone.

He picked up the phone and called Thomas Hale's number again. It went to voicemail again. He did not leave a message.

Instead he opened the back door and looked out at the white blind morning, and said to the empty kitchen, "What do you want me to do?"

The fog said nothing. But on the table the docket book lay open, and Elias understood with a cold and certain clarity, the way a printer understands a page the moment the ink hits it: the notices had never been about the vanished. They were instructions. And the next one would need a printer — would need him — and it was already, somewhere, being set.
