Elias did not sleep. He sat in the kitchen with the docket book open under the lamp and the fog pressed against the glass, and he read the September entry until the letters stopped making sense and became only shapes, the way type does when you have stared at it too long. "Gazette 14 Sept, p.9 low: MISSING notice, Hale. Set from copy rec'd. Paid — Vane & Son account."

The handwriting troubled him more than the words. It was his. Every loop of it was his, the flat 'a' he had carried since his apprenticeship, the dash that always ran a little long. But it was his handwriting from forty years ago, before the arthritis had come into the knuckles, before grief had made his pen drag at the ends of lines. He had written dockets in this hand when Lena was still alive and the shop smelled of hot metal every morning. No man writes in his own young hand. Someone had studied him the way he had once studied type — long enough, close enough, to set him.

And the ink. He touched it with the pad of his thumb and held it to the lamp. It was barely tacky, a month old at most, and he knew then that someone had been in this house, in this kitchen, standing where he stood, while he was up at the archive on his afternoons. The thought should have frightened him. What it did instead was make him angry, a slow printer's anger, the anger of a man whose tools have been taken up by strange hands.

He locked the docket book back in the green cabinet and went upstairs and washed his hands twice, the way he used to after a run of bad ink.

At seven he was on Mrs. Hale's doorstep. The fog had thinned to a white glare and the gulls were screaming over the pier. He had known Margaret Hale for twenty years, the way you know people in a town the size of Greyport — at a distance, through other people's news. She opened the door in a cardigan with the cuffs pulled over her hands, and when she saw him her face did something he would not forget, a small rearrangement of hope and disappointment, as if she had been expecting someone else.

"Mr. Vane," she said. "Have you heard from him?"

"That's why I've come, Margaret. May I come in?"

Her kitchen was like his, only smaller: the same calendar from the chandlery, the same kettle. She poured tea without asking. Her hands shook a little. On the table lay a folded page torn from a notebook, and Elias knew what it was before he touched it, the way you know a mis-set line before you read it.

"He got a call on Tuesday night," she said. "From the Gazette office. Or someone saying it was the office. They said a camera had been left at the desk — a good camera, an expensive one — and they thought it might be his, from the photography. He went down there after supper. He never came home."

Elias looked at the note. Thomas's writing, hurried: "Mum — going to the office. Back by ten. Don't wait up. T."

"Did the police—" Elias began, and stopped. Of course the police. "What did they say?"

"That grown men go missing for their own reasons. That he'll come home." She wrapped the cardigan tighter. "Mr. Vane, you work there. Did you see him?"

The lie formed and died in the same breath. "Not that evening," he said, which was true enough to be a sin. "Margaret, did he say anything before Tuesday? Anything about the archive, or about notices, or—"

"Notices?" Her eyes sharpened. "What notices?"

He had no answer that would not sound like madness, so he drank his tea and told her he would ask around, and she walked him to the door and held his sleeve a moment too long, the way Lena used to when she wanted to say something and couldn't find the words.

The Gazette office was empty on Sunday mornings. The editor did not come in; the night girl had the weekend off; the building stood at the end of the pier street with its shutters down like a closed face. Elias had a key. Three afternoons a week, five years retired — of course he had a key. He let himself in through the side door and stood a moment in the smell of the place, toner and old paper, and told himself he was only going to look at the current files, to see whether the September notice had come through the office in the ordinary way.

It had not, of course. He knew that before he sat down at the desk terminal and searched the September issue files. The notice was nowhere in the digital record. It existed only in the bound volume downstairs and in the issue that had gone to the binder — the physical record, the thing you could touch, the thing that would outlive the servers. Whoever was doing this understood something the young editor never would: that history is not what is published. History is what is bound.

He was halfway down the basement stairs when he heard it. A sound from the bindery room at the far end of the archive — not a sound, really, a change in the air, the way a room feels different when someone has just left it. The light was on. It should not have been on.

Elias stood on the stairs with his hand on the rail and thought about being sixty-three and alone in a basement, and then he thought about Thomas Hale calling him Mr. Vane, and he went down.

The bindery had been disused for years. The guillotine stood under its dust sheet; the stitching machine was a rusted monument. But in the corner, under the work lamp, stood the old imposing stone — the heavy steel table where formes were locked up in the days when the Gazette was set by hand — and on it, gleaming faintly under the lamp, was a chase. A steel frame. And inside the frame, type.

Elias's heart did something it had not done in years. He went to the stone as if he were wading, and looked down at the locked-up page.

It was a classified notice, set in eight-point, plain as a stone. The type was fresh-set, the ink on the proof beside it still wet enough to shine. He read it once, and the basement tilted.

MISSING: one elderly printer, name of Elias Vane, last seen at the archive, Saturday last. His wife is dead and asks after him. Enquire at this office.

His wife is dead and asks after him.

The cruelty of it was precise, surgical — whoever had written this knew about Lena, knew the wound by name. Elias put both hands on the edge of the imposing stone to keep from falling, and the metal was cold through his palms, the cold of the old days, the cold of the shop in winter.

Then he saw the proof sheet beside the chase. Someone had pulled a proof, and on the proof, in pencil, in his own young handwriting, was a single word: "Saturday."

Today was Saturday. This morning was Saturday morning.

He heard the door at the top of the stairs close. Not slam — close, gently, the way you close a door when you do not want to be heard. The lock turned. He was sure of it, the small metallic click that every printer knows, the sound of a forme being locked, of a chase being shut, of a page being made ready.

Elias stood in the yellow light of the work lamp with his own epitaph set in type before him, and for the first time in forty years he understood a page the way the reader was meant to understand it — not as a printer, but as the man it was about. The notices had never been about the vanished. They were instructions. And he had one instruction left to follow.

He reached for the chase, and the type was warm.
