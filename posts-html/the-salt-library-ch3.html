Maya had never been afraid of the dark. Her mother had raised her, in the summers she spent at Gullpoint, to understand that darkness was a tool, like a knife: it cut both ways, and the cut depended on whose hand held it. But walking the causeway at midnight, with the tide at its lowest ebb and the wet sand sucking at her boots, she learned that there was another kind of dark — the kind that listened.

The chalk word on the lamp housing had not washed away. She had tried to rub it off with her sleeve, half in shame, half in defiance, and it had stayed, white as bone against the iron. LIGHT. She did not know whose hand had written it — hers, or her mother's, using her hand the way a musician uses an instrument that remembers the song.

Tomas had told her not to go. He had come to the cottage at nine, pounding on the door, and begged her, and when begging failed he had grown angry, the way old men do when they are frightened.

"You don't understand what you're walking into," he had said. "Your mother — she knew the price of that lamp, and she paid it every night for twenty-eight years. You light it for the Meridian Star and the sea will not take your father. There is no father left to take. It will take you."

"I know," Maya had said.

"You don't know. You think death is the worst price. It isn't. The sea is exact, girl, and it prefers its payments living."

She had not asked what he meant. She had been afraid of the answer.

Now, on the reef path, with the lighthouse black behind her and the lamp room empty of light, she turned the words over. The sea prefers its payments living. Her mother had lived in that tower for twenty-eight years. Her mother had kept the dark nights. Her mother had written thirty-one ledgers in a hand that grew ragged, and stopped a brass clock at 4:17, and whispered "Not tonight. Not this one. Forgive me" to a lamp she was not allowed to light.

The reef was a long black finger of rock reaching into the water. Maya had played there as a child, picking periwinkles, never understanding why her mother always called her back before the tide turned. Now she stood at its end and looked out at the sea, and the sea looked back.

It did not rise in a wave. It rose the way a sleeper rises: slowly, and with intention. The water lifted in a smooth column, ten feet from the rock, and the collector stepped out of it as if it were a doorway, dry this time, his oilskin coat black against the starlight.

"You came," he said.

"You said the price is named at midnight."

He tilted his head, studying her the way a clerk studies a ledger. "The price is named when the keeper is ready to hear it. Midnight is only when the sea does its arithmetic."

Maya's hands were shaking. "Name it."

The collector was silent for a long moment. Somewhere far out, a bell buoy tolled, three times, slow. "Three hundred souls on the Meridian Star," he said. "The Board has signed its order. The reef has been promised. If the light stays dark, the ship dies clean and the debt is paid and you walk away from Gullpoint with your cottage sold and your hands clean."

"And if I light it?"

"Then the sea is cheated of what it was promised." His eyes were the colour of deep water. "And the sea, being exact, will take its equivalent."

Tomas had said it would take her life. Maya braced herself. "Take it, then."

The collector smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "You misunderstand the accounts, keeper's daughter. The sea does not want you dead. The dead pay nothing. The sea wants you keeping."

"Your mother owed twenty-eight years of kept darkness," he said. "When she lit the lamp for the Ostra Light, she broke the terms, and the sea collected — her husband, and in the end her heart. But the debt of the darkness itself was never paid. It only changed hands. It is in the ledgers, in the tower. It is waiting for a keeper." He stepped closer. The air smelled of salt and cold iron. "Light the Meridian Star, and the debt becomes yours. All of it. Every ship. Every dark night. You will keep the lamp dark when the Board orders it, year after year, and you will write it all down in your mother's hand, and you will not leave this island — not for a day, not for an hour — until the sea releases you, which is when you die. And then the ledger will write the next name itself, as it wrote yours."

Maya felt the rock tilt under her feet. "And if I refuse? If I sell the cottage and get on the ferry and never come back?"

"Then the Meridian Star rounds the point in two nights, and the light stays dark, because there is no keeper to light it." He spread his hands. "The reef will do the rest. Three hundred souls, keeper's daughter. And their names will go in the ledger, and the sea will remember that you let it happen, and the debt — the old debt, your mother's debt — will wait. It is patient. It waited a hundred years for your family. It can wait for you to come back, or for your children, or for your children's children."

The bell buoy tolled again. Maya thought of the word she had written on the lamp housing. LIGHT. She thought of her mother's ragged hand in the 1996 ledger: I could not watch. She thought of the brass clock, stopped at 4:17 for thirty years — a memorial to a minute. She had always assumed 4:17 was the minute her father died. Now she wondered if it was something else entirely. The minute her mother had stopped being a daughter and become a keeper. The minute the sea had named its price.

"Is there another way?" she asked. Her voice sounded small against the water. "My mother found one, once. She lit the lamp for the Ostra Light."

"She did." The collector's voice softened, almost gentle. "And the sea collected her husband for forty-one souls, and her heart for the rest, and her daughter's childhood for the interest. That is the other way, keeper's daughter. The sea always collects. The only choice is what you offer."

He began to dissolve, the way mist dissolves — not all at once, but edge by edge. "Two nights," he said, and his voice came from the water now, from everywhere. "The lamp must be lit by your hand, on the hand crank, before she rounds the point. Or not. Choose."

The water settled. The reef was empty. The bell buoy tolled its three slow notes, and then was silent.

Maya walked back along the causeway with the wind at her back, and her mind was a ledger with two columns, and both of them were red. Light the lamp: become the keeper, lose her life to the tower, write ledgers until she died. Let the ship die: three hundred souls on the reef, their names in the ledger, the debt waiting for her children — for children she did not yet have, for a future the sea was already spending.

She was halfway to the cottage when she saw it.

The sitting-room window was lit.

There was no power to the cottage. The Board had cut the cable to the lighthouse, and the cottage ran off the same line — she had lit candles her first night, had cursed the dark kitchen. But the window glowed warm and yellow, lamplight, the colour of oil and wick.

Maya stopped. The wind moved in the grass. Somewhere above, in the tower, she heard a sound she knew from childhood summers: the slow, stately turning of the great lens on its hand crank. Someone was in the lamp room. Someone was keeping the light.

She ran. She threw open the cottage door and the lamplight spilled over her, and the sitting room was warm, and at the kitchen table sat her mother.

Not the old woman from the funeral. The young one, from the photograph on the mantel — dark hair, sea-grey eyes, the keeper's oilskin folded over the back of her chair. She was writing in a ledger, the 1996 book, COLLECTED, and her pen did not stop when Maya came in.

"You came back too soon," her mother said, without looking up. "The price isn't finished being named, Maya. Sit down. There is a third column in the ledger, and no one has ever shown it to you."
