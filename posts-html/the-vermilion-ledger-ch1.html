🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 1 · The Vermilion Ledger

Nihar Banerjee counted everything.

It was not a hobby. It was a habit that thirty-one years in the accounts branch of the Kolkata Police had hammered into him so thoroughly that three years of retirement had not managed to loosen it. He counted the forty-two steps from his gate to the Shyambazar tram depot. He counted the nineteen marigolds along the courtyard wall that his late wife Malati had planted with her own hands, two of them wilting now, and he counted those too, with a small private grief. He counted the days since anybody had knocked on his door carrying a problem that needed solving.

One thousand and ninety-six. Until this morning.

The knock came at half past seven, while the milk was still warming on the stove and the morning newspaper lay folded and unread on the veranda chair. Nihar opened the door to find a thin boy of nineteen or twenty in a faded delivery-company shirt, holding a parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, the way parcels used to be tied before everything came in plastic.

"Banerjee? Nihar Banerjee?" the boy said, squinting at a slip in his hand.

"Yes."

"Sign here." The boy thrust a small green receipt book at him. "Postage paid in cash. No sender name, uncle. The man said you would know."

"What man?"

But the boy was already swinging onto his bicycle. "Old man. Bowbazar side. Gave me fifty rupees extra to bring it before eight." And he was gone, bell jingling, into the morning stream of cycles and handcarts.

Nihar carried the parcel inside. It was heavy, the weight of a brick, the size of a school slate. He cut the string with his penknife, folded back the brown paper, and stopped breathing for a moment.

It was a ledger. Bound in leather dyed so deep a red it was almost black at the edges. The red of sindoor. The red of vermilion, the powder Malati had pressed into the parting of her hair every morning for thirty-eight years. His fingers remembered that colour before his mind did.

Tucked beneath the cover was a slip of paper, folded once. The handwriting on it was small, neat, mercilessly upright, the hand of a man who had kept books all his life.

"You were the only one who ever added it up right."

No signature. None was needed. Nihar knew that hand. He had spent eleven months, back in 2019, trying to untangle its arithmetic across three seized diaries and two half-burnt registers, and he had failed, and the failure had followed him into retirement like a debt of his own.

Madan Mohan Saha. Moneylender of Bowbazar. Missing for twenty-two days.

Nihar set the ledger on the dining table, Malati's dining table, its polish worn pale where her elbows had rested for decades, and washed his hands before touching it further, the way he had always done with evidence. Old rituals die harder than old men. Then he opened it to the first page.

The inside was everything the cover promised. Page after page of entries in that same upright hand, each line ruled straight enough to shame a schoolmaster. Date. Name. Amount. Interest. Due date. Repayment. Saha had lent the way other men breathed, and he had written it all down, because a moneylender who does not write is a moneylender who does not get paid.

Nihar turned the pages slowly, and the Bowbazar of the last two years rose up out of the columns. Haradhan Mistanna Bhandar, sweet-shop, forty thousand, repaid in eleven instalments. Rafiq the taxi driver, sixty thousand for an engine overhaul, repaid with interest to the last rupee. A printing-press owner in College Street. A widow in Mirza Ghalib Street who had borrowed for her daughter's wedding and repaid early, with a note in the margin in a different pen: good woman, reduce interest.

It was all so ordinary that Nihar almost smiled. Almost.

Then he reached page thirty-one, and the smile died.

Eleven entries in the book were marked with a thumbprint in red ink, pressed hard beside the borrower's name. Against each, in the margin, a single word in Saha's hand: recovered. Paid in full, in cash, every one of them, on dates scattered across the last six months.

Nihar's eye, trained on thirty-one years of cooked books, caught the wrongness in under a minute. He fetched his reading glasses and a pencil, and he added, the way he had added ten thousand times before.

Three of the eleven names belonged to men Nihar knew to be dead.

Tapan Ghosh, aide to a Bowbazar councillor, found at the foot of the Sealdah flyover last month. Ruled an accident. The newspapers had given it four lines. His entry in the ledger said: two lakh, forty thousand. Recovered in full, in cash, nine days before he died.

Nihar's pencil stopped moving. He turned back through the pages, checking the running totals the way a doctor checks a pulse, and the deeper wrongness surfaced underneath the first one. The eleven thumbprint loans were marked recovered, but the recoveries never appeared in any cash column. The money came in and vanished off the page, as if eleven men had paid with their lives and Saha had simply written recovered over the graves.

No. That was melodrama, and Nihar distrusted melodrama the way he distrusted round numbers. But the arithmetic did not care about his distrust. The arithmetic said: eleven loans, eleven thumbprints, eleven recoveries that existed nowhere except in red ink.

He was so deep in the columns that he did not hear the scooter at first. Then the sound registered, the particular uneven idle of a two-stroke engine left running, and Nihar looked up at the window.

A scooter stood at his gate. The rider wore a helmet with the visor down, though the morning was already warm. For perhaps twenty seconds the rider simply sat there, engine running, facing the house. Then the scooter pulled away, unhurried, and turned the corner toward the tram depot.

Nihar was at the veranda in eleven strides. The lane was empty. But in the dust by the gatepost, a cigarette stub still smouldered, a thin ribbon of smoke rising straight up in the windless air.

Nihar, who counted everything, bent down and picked it up between two fingers. Filter tip. Expensive brand. Still warm.

Whoever had been watching had left eleven seconds ago. And on the last written page of the red ledger, below the final entry, in ink so fresh it had not fully dried when the parcel was wrapped, Madan Mohan Saha had written one more line.

"Page forty-seven is missing. Find it before they do."

Nihar turned to page forty-seven. The stub of it was still in the binding, a pale tongue of paper with a razored edge.

Somewhere in Bowbazar, a page was walking around loose with eleven red thumbprints on it, or the secret behind them. And somebody, Nihar understood with a coldness settling low in his stomach, had just finished counting his house the way he counted everything else.

He turned the stub over in his fingers. Expensive. Filter tip, gold band. The kind of cigarette a man smokes when he wants you to know he can afford it. Nihar dropped it into an empty matchbox and slipped the matchbox into his pocket. Evidence. Old rituals.

Then he looked at the brown paper still clutched in his other hand, the wrapping the delivery boy had left with him, and saw what he should have seen at the kitchen table. In one corner, half torn away where the string had cut it, was the ghost of a rubber stamp. He tilted it to the light. Two words in faded blue ink, the second half missing: "Saha &" — and below it, unmistakable even torn: "Bowbazar".

So the parcel had come from Saha's own shop. From the moneylender's own counter, wrapped by hands that knew brown paper and string. A man missing for twenty-two days does not wrap parcels. Which meant someone else had wrapped it. Someone who wanted Nihar Banerjee to have the red ledger. Someone who was now counting his house the way he counted everything else.

He went inside, locked the door, and began to read the ledger from the beginning, the way a man reads a map of a minefield.
