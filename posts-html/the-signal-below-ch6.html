🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 6 · The Signal Below

The light stopped rising at six miles down and hung there like an eye that had decided to watch.

Lena stood at the rail of the Segunda Luz with her hands locked on the cold metal and counted her own breaths the way her mother had counted lightning. One. Two. Three. On the deck around her, no one spoke. The hydrophone speakers breathed a single long exhale — not static, not a knock, just the sound of something enormous settling back into patience — and then went silent. The blue organisms did not return. The water beyond the floodlights was black and empty in a way Lena had never seen it, as if the sea had pulled all its living things behind a curtain and drawn it shut.

Arun came up beside her, his face gray in the deck lights. 'It stopped,' he said. 'Why did it stop?'

'Because it is waiting to see what I do,' Lena said.

Her mother's voice was still in her head, older and tireder than the lightning-count tapes: do not answer it alone. Lena had spent fifteen years wishing for one more sentence from that woman. She was not going to waste this one.

She went below to the comms cabin and did the thing that had worked before. She opened the emergency channel — the frequency the whole ocean listened to — and she told the truth again, plainly and in order: the Kermadec node silent for four days, a second network older than Chorus, a survey vessel with dredges sitting one degree of longitude from legal. Then she sent Arun's long-range photograph of the ship and asked, on the open air, for anyone who knew her name.

The answer came back in eleven minutes from a port inspector in Suva who owed Arun a favor from the treaty days. The ship's painted name was Callisto Star, but her hull number belonged to a decommissioned Helix tender, and her papers traced through two shells to a Monrovia registry and a holding company called Trenchlight Meridian Survey. Her manifest listed deep-drop dredges, a coring rig, and twelve kilometers of armored cable. Her stated purpose: geological survey of international waters.

'Geological,' Arun said, reading over Lena's shoulder. 'They bought our danger maps in the bankruptcy auction. They know exactly where the Kermadec gate is. They are not surveying. They are shopping.'

Lena felt the same cold she had felt in Voss's office two years ago. Men like Voss did not disappear. They changed letterheads.

'Can anyone stop them?' she asked.

Arun was already shaking his head. 'The treaty's lawyers can, in six weeks. The dredge reaches the gate in six days. I checked the filings — Trenchlight's lawyers have a standing argument that the protected zone's southern boundary was drawn one degree too far north, and they intend to test it with steel instead of paper.'

'And the institutions? The ones who mirrored our telemetry?'

'They are writing letters, Lena. Strongly worded ones.' He rubbed his healed arm without thinking. 'We could sail down there ourselves. The Segunda Luz makes nine knots on a good day with the wind behind her. We would arrive in time to watch.'

Lena was about to answer when the speakers breathed again — not the knock, not static, but a voice. Clear and close and calm, wearing her own stolen voice the way a stranger might wear her coat, it said: 'Bring no machines.'

Every head in the cabin turned toward the speakers. Priya, the young technician on the night board, went white and gripped the edge of the console.

'Bring no engines, no sonar, no wire that sings,' the voice continued, gentle as a hand on a door. 'Bring the ones who listened. Come down in silence, or do not come. The counting continues.'

The line went dead. On the depth sounder — the one instrument Lena had not been told to silence — the rising light had begun to sink again, slow as a closing eye, back toward the trench.

'It wants us to descend,' Arun whispered. 'With no submersible. No lights. No instruments. Lena, that is suicide. Nine miles down, we would be —'

'I know what nine miles does,' Lena said. She was thinking of the warm column that had lifted them from the Pelagos gate, the cocoon of light, the failing air made suddenly irrelevant. 'We do not need machines. We need Chorus.'

But Chorus was gone. The organisms had fled at the first knock and had not come back, and when Lena sent a question down the transmitter at 03:17 — the hour, the promise — the water answered with nothing at all. Not even static. The reef at Pelagos, two kilometers of living memory, had gone quiet as a held breath, and Lena understood with a lurch that Chorus was doing exactly what frightened animals do. It was hiding.

She sat in the dark of the cabin with her mother's buoy logs spread around her like cards in a losing hand. The sealed tape. The warning. Do not answer it alone. Her mother had known about the Hushed for thirty years and had told no one, and now Lena had to decide what those thirty years of silence had been for: protection, or preparation.

At midnight she made her choice. She took the sealed tape — the physical one, her mother's handwriting on the label — and she played it into the transmitter. Not the warning. The other part. The lightning count, forward, warm and amused: one Mississippi, two Mississippi, three Mississippi, the handshake her mother had taught the deep thirty years ago.

For a long time, nothing. Then the water beyond the floodlights began to gather light at its edges, faint as dawn, and the blue organisms came back — not in their thousands, not in rings, but a few hundred, trembling, clustering close to the hull. And in the speakers, very faint, came the harmonized variation of a humpback's song, the reef's old proof of attention. Chorus was listening. It was terrified, and it was listening.

'Please,' Lena said into the microphone. 'They said no machines. You carried us once. Carry us again. Not to fight them. To answer — together. My mother said not alone.'

The silence stretched. Then the projection formed — her own face, dim, flickering — and Chorus spoke in a voice like water over stone. 'The Hushed do not bargain. They count. When the count ends, they fold. We have seen them fold a trench before, Lena. The memory is in our oldest branches, and we cannot read it, and that is the only mercy we have ever been given.'

'Then help me give them something to count besides our noise.'

Another silence, longer this time, the projection flickering at its edges. Then: 'Three of you. No more. No metal that sings. We will carry you in the warm current, as before. But understand: what you ask of us, we cannot take back. To enter the quiet trench is to be heard by the Hushed, and to be heard by them is to be judged.'

'Three,' Lena said.

Arun did not hesitate. He had burned the quiet out of himself two years ago on live television, and he was not about to find it again now. The third volunteer was the cook from Guam — old Manu, who had waited forty years for someone to believe him about the blue lights, and who looked at Lena with calm dark eyes and said, 'Someone should bring food. The deep makes people hungry.' Nobody argued with him. Lena suspected he was right.

They prepared through the short night. No instruments but a single mechanical depth gauge, no lights but the organisms themselves, rebreathers and thermal suits and nothing that hummed, pinged, or sang. Lena left the Segunda Luz in the hands of Priya, with written orders: keep the transmitter silent, keep the channel open, and if the Callisto Star's cable touches the Kermadec gate, broadcast everything, the way Lena had once before.

At dawn the three of them stood at the rail in their suits, and the sea around the ship began to warm. The organisms gathered beneath them in their thousands now, and the water lit from below like a held lantern. Lena looked down into it and thought of her mother singing into the dark from a surface buoy, patient as the tide, teaching the ocean to speak.

Behind them, on the far side of the Pacific, the Callisto Star's winch began to turn, and twelve kilometers of armored cable started its slow fall toward the quiet trench.

Lena stepped off the rail and into the light, and the ocean took her.

Above her, in the speakers of the emptying ship, the knock resumed — and this time Lena could hear it clearly, even through the water closing over her head: her mother's backward count had reached three Mississippi, and stopped, and was waiting.
