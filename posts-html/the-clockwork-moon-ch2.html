🔊 Audiobook — Chapter 2 · The Clockwork Moon

The Queen's engineers could not start the machine. Mara watched from the edge of the courtyard crowd as they tried every tool in the palace workshops: fine picks, heavy drivers, even a diamond-toothed saw brought up from the mines. The machine's central lock was a small dark mouth at the heart of the great watch-face, and it was shaped, unmistakably, for the key hidden in Mara's sleeve. Each tool they forced into it melted. The picks came out as drips of silver. The saw blade sagged like warm wax. One young engineer wept with frustration, and his master cuffed him for it, and still the lock waited, patient as a held breath, for its true key.

Mara was trying to decide whether to run when a hand closed gently around her elbow. Captain Cael Arden, the frightened young officer from the observatory, leaned close enough that she could smell lamp-oil and fear on him. "Not here," he breathed. "Walk with me. Slowly. Like we belong together." She let him steer her through the crowd and into the palace, down corridors hung with tapestries gone gray in the frozen lamplight, and into an unused gallery where dust lay thick on the sills and a row of covered portraits stood like patient ghosts.

"They'll search everyone before the night ends," Cael said, shutting the door. He was younger than his uniform, Mara saw now: perhaps twenty, with ink stains on his fingers that no captain should have. "Listen to me. The Queen means to return to a single day—the day her son died." He spoke quickly, as if the words hurt coming out. "Prince Dain. Fourteen years old. The Old Bridge collapsed during the spring parade, and he was on it. I was his guard." His hands twisted together. "I was nineteen. I carried him from the river, and he was already gone, and the Queen has never forgiven the world for it. Or me. Or herself."

Mara's throat tightened. "And the machine?"

"My guilt helped build it," Cael said bitterly. "I brought the Queen Master Orin's old theories about lunar time. I thought—if we could go back, I could stand on that bridge and shout the warning in time." He laughed, a short unhappy sound. "Then the calculations came back. Reversing one year doesn't just return the dead. It unmakes everything that happened after. Every child born since that day would never have been born. Every marriage, every harvest, every song written in twelve months—erased, as if the year had never turned." He met her eyes. "When I understood, I began to resist. Quietly. Master Orin discovered the same danger independently. He went up to the lunar gear to sabotage it, and he never came back down."

Mara studied him. His fear at the observatory had been real; his grief now was real. But grief, she was learning, made people into strangers. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the lock is shaped for your key," Cael said simply. "The bird gave it to you. Orin's bird. Which means Orin chose you, and I need you to trust me for one hour." He crossed to the far wall and pressed a panel that swung inward without a sound, revealing a dark shaft smelling of hot metal. "A service tunnel. It runs beneath the machine. If we're going to stop this, we start there."

She didn't trust him. She followed him anyway, because the alternative was the Queen's searchers and the key in her sleeve, and because Orin's bird had said choose when you must. The tunnel was narrow and breathed heat. They crawled beneath spinning shafts that turned without turning, the whole machine straining against stopped time, and emerged into a chamber that made Mara gasp aloud.

Bottled seconds. Hundreds of them, ranked on shelves from floor to ceiling: small glass bulbs, each glowing faintly, each containing a stolen moment. Mara pressed her face close to one and heard, impossibly small, a child's laughter at a birthday table. Another held rain drumming on shutters. Another, an argument between lovers, the words blurred but the ache intact. And one, dark at its core, held a final breath, exhaled in a sickroom, kept like a jewel. "The engineers harvest them," Cael whispered. "Moments of strong feeling, taken from citizens without their knowing. Joy and grief both burn hot. The machine drinks them to power the reversal." Mara moved slowly along the shelves, and then she stopped, her breath catching, before a bulb no brighter than a candle flame. Pressed close, she heard it: a woman humming, low and sweet, a lullaby half-remembered from the edge of sleep. Her mother's lullaby. Mara's hands shook. They had taken even this—this private, buried thing—and hung it on a shelf like preserves. She thought of the bakers waiting for dawn, the eel-seller weeping in the lane, and understood that the city was being unmade one memory at a time to feed the Queen's yesterday. Fifteen years she had mourned without that melody, and here it glowed in stolen glass, burning to power another woman's grief.

Beyond the chamber of seconds lay the pendulum room, and there they found Master Orin.

He was imprisoned within the great pendulum's cage, and the pendulum still swung—impossibly, in the stopped night—and each swing, Cael whispered, aged him by a day. Orin's hair had gone white. His hands shook. But his eyes were clear, and when he saw Mara he smiled the exact smile he wore when she finally mastered a difficult escapement. "My clever girl," he said. "You followed the smallest gear."

Mara jammed her wrench into the pendulum's ratchet while Cael attacked the cage lock with a pry bar, and between them the mechanism shuddered and held, shuddered and held, and then gave way with a scream of tortured brass. Orin stumbled out and caught himself against Mara's shoulder, light as a bundle of sticks. "The key," he rasped. "Show me."

She opened her palm. In the pendulum room's cold light the iron key seemed to drink the glow from the bottled seconds on the walls. "It does two things," Orin said. "Turned one way in the lunar socket, it winds the moon backward—one year, as the Queen intends, unmaking all that followed. Turned the other way, it releases every captured moment at once. Time restarts. But the stored seconds will return unpredictably." His grip tightened on her wrist. "Forgotten grief may strike without warning, Mara. Lost joys may visit strangers. The city will be changed—oh, it will be changed—but not erased. It will still be itself."

Before Mara could answer, the gallery door crashed open. The Queen entered with six guards, and she carried no scepter, only a miniature portrait in a silver frame, held against her heart like a wound. She looked at Orin, at Cael, at Mara with the key gleaming in her open hand, and her face did not change. "Clockmaker," she said softly. "Have you ever lost someone worth breaking the world to recover?"

Mara thought of her mother, dead of river fever before Mara was old enough to learn the names of tools, before she ever made a clock that kept true time. She thought of the dented compass on the workshop shelf, keeping its own time, and of all the mornings she had woken reaching for a voice that wasn't there. For one aching second, yesterday felt like mercy. For one second, she understood the Queen completely, and the key felt warm in her palm, eager, ready to turn backward.

Then, on the shelf behind the Queen, a bottled moment cracked.

It was a small sound, like ice breaking on a puddle, and from the fractured bulb rose the unmistakable bright cry of a newborn taking its first breath—startled, furious, alive. The sound filled the pendulum room, and every guard lowered his eyes, and the Queen's hand trembled on the portrait frame. Somewhere in Lume, Mara thought, a mother was missing this exact cry and did not know why.

Mara closed her fist around the key. "The future is not ours to spend," she said.
