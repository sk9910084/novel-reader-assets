Orin listened to the bulb for a long time, his ear pressed to the glass, his white head bowed over the workbench, and when he finally set it down his hands were trembling worse than usual. "Recent," he said quietly. "Days old, not years. And the glasswork—" He turned the bulb in the lamplight, frowning at the seal. "Mara. This is palace glass. This was blown in the Queen's own workshops, by the same hands that made the first harvest."

"The machine was dismantled," Mara said. "We watched the engineers take it apart."

"The machine was dismantled." Orin set the bulb down with terrible gentleness. "The knowledge was not. You cannot un-teach a hundred engineers, my clever girl. And grief, once someone has learned to price it—" He did not finish. He did not need to.

Cael Arden arrived at dusk, as he did most evenings now, with sawdust on his boots and the bridge commission's drawings under his arm. He had built the new bridge with every warning carved into its stone, and children traced the letters on their way to school, and Lume loved him for it. But when Mara told him about the moon-mongers' stall, the warmth drained from his face, and he looked twenty years old and terrified again, the way he had looked at the observatory on the night the moon stopped.

"Show me," he said.

They went to Marrow Lane at first light, but the black stall was gone. The fair had moved on in the night, as fairs do, leaving only trampled grass and a dropped brass token in the gutter where the stall had stood. Mara picked it up: a small coin, stamped with a gear-tooth, warm to the touch though the morning was cold. Cael turned it over in his palm, and his jaw tightened. "Moon-gold," he said. "The palace mint struck these for the cable-works, back when we were building the machine. Payment for silence. They were all supposed to be melted down."

The laundress was not hard to find. Her name was Petra, and she lived in two rooms above the wash-house on Tanners' Row, and when Mara knocked she opened the door with the bulb from the fair clutched in one hand and the look of someone who has not slept. She let them in. On the table stood a row of bulbs, six of them, dim and guttering.

"They said I could trade," Petra whispered. "They said the borrowed joy would last me through the winter, and all I had to give was memories I was tired of carrying." She picked up the largest bulb, and her hand shook. "My wedding day. I sold them my wedding day. And Tomas's voice, the way he said my name." A tear ran down her cheek. "The joy fades, Keeper. It always fades by morning, like they said. But the memories don't come back. I reached for my husband's face last night, and there was—" Her voice broke. "There was a shape, where his face should be. A shape with no face in it."

Mara knelt beside her and took her cold hands, and thought of the shelf in the service tunnel, of her own mother's lullaby glowing in stolen glass, of fifteen years of mourning a melody. "We will get them back," she said, and heard the promise ringing hollow even as she spoke it, because she did not know if it was true. Stolen joy returned unpredictably. Stolen grief might be gone entirely, fed to something growing on the moon.

"The grief is the key," Orin said that night, when they told him. He sat by the workshop fire with a blanket over his knees, older than his years, sharper than ever. "Think, Mara. The borrowed joys fade because joy refuses to be property—it will not sit still in glass. But grief keeps. Grief is patient. Grief waits to be useful. Someone is buying grief the way the Queen once bought seconds, and we must ask what the grief is feeding." He looked at her, and his eyes were clear as the day she had mastered her first escapement. "Follow the grief, my clever girl. It will lead you to the tooth."

They followed it to the river camps, three nights later, past the last lamp of the outer walls. Cael went first, unarmed, in a laborer's coat, because he would not bring guards to frighten folk who had nothing left to frighten. Mara went beside him with a wrench in her sleeve and the Lunar Key cold against her wrist, though she had not climbed to the moon in two years. The moon-mongers' camp was a ring of wagons around a fire that burned too steady in the windless dark. And in the center of the ring stood a cradle of brass and cable, no bigger than a cradle for a child, humming faintly. Inside it, cradled in velvet, grew a black tooth the size of a man's knuckle. Matte. Lightless. Vibrating. And around it, ranked on shelves built into the wagons, hundreds of dark bulbs glowed with harvested grief. Mara moved past them slowly, her lantern raised, and heard fragments as she passed: a widow's last argument, an old man's final breath in a sickroom, a child's scream cut short by water. Each one fresh. Each one taken from someone living, someone who would wake tomorrow with a hollow place where the grief had been, and call it healing, and never know what they had sold.

The man tending the cradle had been one of the Queen's cable-masters. Mara recognized him from the courtyard that first night: Varric, gray now, thin as wire, his hands moving over the mechanism with the terrible tenderness of a father over a sick child.

"You know what it is," he said without looking up, as if he had been expecting them all along. "Grief made solid. The Queen fed it her own sorrow and it nearly unmade the world. So they broke it." He laughed, a dry sound like paper tearing. "But they never asked where broken grief goes, did they? It goes into the dark. It waits. And when the city forgot—when everyone forgave and moved on and started building bridges with warnings carved in stone—" His voice cracked. "My boy died in the spring floods, Keeper. Eleven years old. The new bridge came too late for him. And the city had no grief left to give me, because the city had healed. So I went looking for what the city threw away."

Cael stepped forward, his hands open. "Varric. This will grow. You know it will grow. It will not stop at a cradle."

"Let it grow," Varric whispered. "Let it grow until it is big enough to hold every grief that has nowhere to go. Until it is big enough to give them back." He laid his hand on the black tooth, and it pulsed under his palm like a second heart. And then it spoke.

It spoke with a woman's voice, low and sweet, humming. A lullaby half-remembered from the edge of sleep.

Mara's mother's lullaby.

Mara's knees went weak. "That is mine," she whispered. "That was returned to me. That was—how does it know that song?"

Varric looked at her then, and his eyes were full of a pity that frightened her more than anything the moon had ever done. "The threads, Keeper," he said softly. "The silver threads still tie every heart to the moon. Nothing sung under this sky is ever truly lost. The tooth only has to listen." He turned back to the cradle. "It listens to all of us now. And it is learning our voices."

High above the river camps, the real moon turned in its slow majesty, and at its rim the black tooth grew by a fraction, and Mara felt it through the soles of her feet, a lurch and a wrongness she knew too well. And in the workshop on the lane, on its plain nail between a rusted spring and the Lunar Key, her mother's dented compass—her mother's compass, which had begun keeping true time again two years ago—stopped.
